Maths Toolkit: Areas, Small Elements & Integration

Electrostatics deals with very big and very small numbers. The charge of an electron is 1.6 × 10−19 C, and k is 9 × 10⁹ N m² C−2. You must handle powers of ten and SI prefixes without slips. Gauss's law needs the areas of spheres and cylinders. Charged rods and sheets are handled by cutting them into small elements and adding them up (∑ → ∫). Field lines are explained with the solid angle (ഘനകോൺ). This page builds each of these tools from scratch.

📖 What you need
  • Multiplying and dividing decimals; area of a rectangle = length × breadth.
  • π ≈ 3.14 (or 22/7). Nothing about calculus is assumed: the integration you need is built up here.

1. Powers of ten and SI prefixes

Write very large and very small numbers in scientific notation: a number between 1 and 10 times a power of ten. For example 0.000 003 C = 3 × 10−6 C and 9 000 000 000 = 9 × 10⁹. Three rules do almost all the work:

10a × 10b = 10a+b  ·  10a ÷ 10b = 10a−b  ·  (10a)² = 102a

The coulomb is a very large unit, so charges are usually given with a prefix. Always convert to coulombs and metres before you substitute.

🧪 Prefix converter – and how many electrons is that?

Number of electrons n = q ÷ e with e = 1.6 × 10−19 C. Even 1 pC (10−12 C) is about 6 million electrons, and 1 C is about 6 × 1018 electrons. You will use this in section 1.5 (quantisation of charge).

Worked example 1 · Powers of ten in one line

Q. Evaluate (9 × 10⁹) × (2 μC) × (3 μC) ÷ (30 cm)². (This is exactly the calculation you will do with Coulomb's law; the answer is a force in newtons.)

Convert first: 2 μC = 2 × 10−6 C, 3 μC = 3 × 10−6 C, 30 cm = 0.3 m, so (0.3)² = 0.09 = 9 × 10−2. Multiply the plain numbers and add the powers of ten separately.

🎬 Exam layout, step by step
Answer: (9 × 2 × 3 ÷ 9) × 109 − 6 − 6 + 2 = 6 × 10−1 = 0.6 (N).

Gauss's law (sections 1.14–1.15) multiplies a field by the area of a closed surface. Learn these five formulas:

🧪 Shapes for Gaussian surfaces

Double the radius of a sphere and its area becomes 4 times larger (area ∝ r²). That single fact is why the field of a point charge falls as 1/r²: the same field lines spread over 4 times the area. Areas are in m², so 10 cm is first written as 0.1 m.

Worked example 2 · Area of a Gaussian sphere

Q. A sphere of radius 10 cm surrounds a charge. The field on its surface is 900 N/C, pointing straight outward everywhere. Find (a) the area of the sphere and (b) the product E × area (this product is the flux, section 1.10).

r = 10 cm = 0.1 m. Area = 4πr². Then multiply by 900.

🎬 Exam layout, step by step
Answer: (a) 4π(0.1)² = 0.04π ≈ 0.126 m²; (b) 900 × 0.1257 ≈ 113 N m²/C.

3. Small elements and summing: ∑ → ∫

Coulomb's law is for point charges. What about a charged rod? Cut it into many tiny pieces of length Δl. Each piece is so small that it behaves like a point charge ΔQ = λ Δl, where λ (lambda) is the charge per metre (the linear charge density, section 1.13). Find the effect of each piece and add them all: ∑ (capital sigma) means “sum of”.

As the pieces get smaller and more numerous, the sum gets more and more exact. In the limit of infinitely many infinitely thin pieces, the sum ∑ becomes an integral ∫ (a long S for “sum”), and Δl becomes dl:

Q = ∑ λ Δl  →  Q = ∫ λ dl   ·   area under a graph y(x) from a to b = ∑ y Δx → ∫ab y dx
🧪 Thinner and thinner strips: the area under y = 1/x²

Each strip has width Δx = (b − 1)/N, and its height is the curve's value at the middle of the strip. Add all the areas y Δx: that is ∑. As N grows the total settles on the exact value ∫1b dx/x² = 1 − 1/b. The function 1/x² is the shape of the Coulomb force and field.

4. The few integrals you need

An integral “undoes” differentiation. For powers of x there is one rule (it works for every n except −1):

∫ xⁿ dx = xⁿ⁺¹ ÷ (n + 1)   ·   ∫ab f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)
  • With n = −2: ∫ dx/x² = x−1/(−1) = −1/x, so ∫ab dx/x² = 1/a − 1/b. (You will see it again in Chapter 2.)
  • ∫ cos θ dθ = sin θ, so ∫−π/2π/2 cos θ dθ = sin(π/2) − sin(−π/2) = 1 − (−1) = 2. This is the key step of exercise 1.30, the field of a long charged wire.
  • ∫ dl = l: if λ is the same everywhere, Q = ∫ λ dl = λL. Likewise Q = σA for a sheet and Q = ρV for a volume.
  • A ring on a closed surface: ∮ (a circle on the ∫ sign) means “sum over the whole closed surface”. Gauss's law is written ∮ E·dS = q/ε₀.

Worked example 3 · Two definite integrals

Q. Evaluate (a) ∫12 dx/x² and (b) ∫−π/2π/2 cos θ dθ.

(a) ∫ dx/x² = −1/x; substitute the upper limit, then subtract the lower. (b) ∫ cos θ dθ = sin θ; sin(π/2) = 1 and sin(−π/2) = −1.

🎬 Exam layout, step by step
Answer: (a) [−1/x]12 = −½ + 1 = ½; (b) [sin θ]−π/2π/2 = 1 − (−1) = 2.

Worked example 4 · Adding up the charge on a rod

Q. A thin rod 50 cm long carries a uniform linear charge density λ = 2 μC/m. (a) What is the charge on a 1 mm piece of it? (b) What is the total charge on the rod?

ΔQ = λ Δl for a small piece; for the whole rod add all the pieces: Q = ∫ λ dl = λL. Convert to metres first.

🎬 Exam layout, step by step
Answer: (a) ΔQ = 2 × 10−6 × 10−3 = 2 × 10−9 C (2 nC); (b) Q = λL = 2 × 10−6 × 0.5 = 1 × 10−6 C (1 μC).

5. Solid angle – the 3-D angle

An ordinary angle in radians is arc length ÷ radius: θ = s/r. A whole circle is 2π radians. The solid angle (ഘനകോൺ) does the same in three dimensions. A cone from a point cuts out an area ΔS on a sphere of radius r around that point. The solid angle is

ΔΩ = ΔS ÷ r²    (unit: steradian, sr)   ·   whole sphere: 4πr² ÷ r² = 4π sr

The same cone cuts out 4 times the area on a sphere of twice the radius, but ΔS/r² stays the same. So the field lines inside a cone are spread over an area that grows as r². Their number per unit area, and with it the field, falls as 1/r². Section 1.9 uses exactly this argument. A cone of half-angle α cuts out a cap of area 2πr²(1 − cos α), so ΔΩ = 2π(1 − cos α).

🧪 One cone, two spheres

Seen side-on, each sphere is a circle and each cap is an arc. The cone carries 6 field lines. They cross the small cap and the big cap alike, but the big cap is 4 times larger, so the lines are 4 times sparser there. At α = 90° the cone becomes a half-space: 2π sr, half of 4π.

⭐ Key ideas
  • μ = 10−6, n = 10−9, p = 10−12; cm → m: ÷ 100; square the distance after converting.
  • Sphere: area 4πr², volume (4/3)πr³; cylinder: curved area 2πrL, ends πr² each; cube: 6a².
  • Cut into small elements (ΔQ = λΔl, σΔS, ρΔV), add them: ∑ → ∫ when the pieces become infinitely thin.
  • ∫ab dx/x² = 1/a − 1/b; ∫−π/2π/2 cos θ dθ = 2; ∫ λ dl = λL for uniform λ.
  • Solid angle ΔΩ = ΔS/r²; whole sphere 4π sr; it does not depend on r, which leads to the 1/r² law.
  • Point → spherical symmetry; line → cylindrical; plane sheet → planar.
