Vectors Toolkit: Components, Unit Vectors & Dot Product

Electric force and electric field have a direction as well as a size. To add them, you must add them as arrows, not as plain numbers. Your textbook writes Coulomb's law as F21 = k q₁q₂ r̂21/r²21, flux as E·ΔS, and torque on a dipole as p × E. This page teaches every vector idea behind those formulas from scratch: what a vector (സദിശം) is, how to add vectors, components, unit vectors (യൂണിറ്റ് സദിശം), position vectors, the dot product and the cross product. You can drag every arrow yourself.

📖 What you need
  • Pythagoras' theorem: in a right triangle, hypotenuse² = base² + height².
  • sin θ, cos θ and tan θ for a right triangle. Remember sin 30° = 0.5, cos 60° = 0.5, cos 30° = sin 60° ≈ 0.866, cos 90° = 0, and cos 0° = 1.

1. Scalars and vectors

A scalar (അദിശം) has only a size (magnitude) with a unit: mass 2 kg, time 5 s, temperature 30 °C, and electric charge 3 μC. Scalars add like ordinary numbers. Charge is a scalar, so a +2 μC charge and a −5 μC charge together make −3 μC.

A vector (സദിശം) has a magnitude and a direction: displacement “3 m east”, velocity, acceleration, force, and in this chapter the electric field, the dipole moment and the area vector. We draw a vector as an arrow. The length of the arrow shows the magnitude and the arrowhead shows the direction. In print a vector is written in bold (F) or with a small arrow drawn over the letter. Its magnitude is written |F| or just F.

2. Adding vectors: triangle and parallelogram laws

Walk 3 m east and then 4 m north. You end up only 5 m from the start, not 7 m. Vectors add by joining arrows:

  • Triangle law (head to tail): draw A, then start B at the head of A. The resultant R = A + B runs from the tail of A to the head of B.
  • Parallelogram law: draw A and B from the same point and complete the parallelogram. The diagonal from that point is R. Both laws give the same answer.

If the angle between A and B is θ, the cosine rule gives the size and the direction of the resultant:

R = √(A² + B² + 2AB cos θ)   ·   tan α = B sin θ ÷ (A + B cos θ)  (α = angle of R from A)

Special cases worth remembering: θ = 0° gives R = A + B (same direction). θ = 180° gives R = |A − B| (opposite directions). θ = 90° gives R = √(A² + B²) (Pythagoras).

🧪 Drag two vectors and see their resultant

Drag the orange dots at the heads of A and B. One grid square is 1 unit; the heads snap to half-units. Make them point the same way (R = A + B), opposite ways (R = A − B) and at right angles (R = √(A² + B²)). In this chapter A and B will be forces or fields from two charges.

Worked example 1 · Two forces at 60°

Q. Two forces of 3 N and 5 N act on a small charged ball. The angle between them is 60°. Find the magnitude of the resultant force and the angle it makes with the 3 N force.

Use R = √(A² + B² + 2AB cos θ) with A = 3 N, B = 5 N, θ = 60° and cos 60° = 0.5. Then use tan α = B sin θ ÷ (A + B cos θ).

🎬 Exam layout, step by step
Answer: R = √(9 + 25 + 15) = √49 = 7 N, at α ≈ 38.2° from the 3 N force (tan α = 4.33 ÷ 5.5 ≈ 0.787).

3. Components and the unit vectors î, ĵ, k̂

Adding arrows by drawing is slow. The quick way is to split every vector into components along the x- and y-axes. A vector A of magnitude A at an angle θ to the x-axis has

Ax = A cos θ    Ay = A sin θ    A = √(Ax² + Ay²)    tan θ = Ay ÷ Ax

A unit vector (യൂണിറ്റ് സദിശം) has magnitude exactly 1 and no unit. Its only job is to show a direction. The three special ones are î (along +x), ĵ (along +y) and k̂ (along +z, out of the page). Any vector can be written as

A = Ax î + Ay ĵ + Az k̂

The unit vector along any vector is that vector divided by its own magnitude: Â = A ÷ A. In particular, for a position vector r, r̂ = r ÷ r. You will see r̂ in every field formula of this chapter. It means “the direction pointing away from the source charge”.

🧪 Components and the unit vector

Drag the orange head of A. The dashed lines are its components Ax and Ay. The red arrow is the unit vector Â: it always ends on the dotted circle of radius 1, however long A is. Changing the length of A changes A but not Â; changing its direction changes both.

Adding by components is just adding the numbers in front of î and in front of ĵ separately: (2î + 3ĵ) + (4î − ĵ) = 6î + 2ĵ. Subtracting works the same way. This is how you will add the forces or fields of several charges (superposition). Find the x- and y-components of each, add, then use Pythagoras.

🧭 î, ĵ and k̂ in three dimensions

The three axes are at right angles to one another and form a right-handed set. Curl the fingers of your right hand from +x towards +y, and your thumb points along +z. On a flat page, +z comes out of the page (drawn ⊙) and −z goes into the page (drawn ⊗).

4. Position vectors and r₂₁ = r₂ − r₁

The position vector r of a point is the arrow from the origin O to that point. If charge q₁ sits at r₁ and charge q₂ at r₂, the arrow from q₁ to q₂ is

r21 = r2 − r1   ·   r21 = |r21|   ·   r̂21 = r21 ÷ r21

Read the subscript “21” as “to 2, from 1”. The arrow r12 = r1 − r2 = −r21 points the other way. In Coulomb's law (section 1.6), the force F21 on q₂ due to q₁ is along r̂21 when the charges repel.

🧪 Two charges, two position vectors

Drag q₁ and q₂. The purple arrow r21 always goes from q₁ to q₂, and r21 = r2 − r1 holds in every position. The short red arrows preview Coulomb's law. Like charges push each other apart: the force on q₂ is along +r̂21 and the force on q₁ is along −r̂21. Unlike charges pull together.

Worked example 2 · Position vectors and a unit vector

Q. Charge q₁ is at the point (1 m, 1 m) and charge q₂ at (4 m, 5 m). Find (a) r21, (b) the distance r21 between them and (c) the unit vector r̂21.

Write r₁ = î + ĵ and r₂ = 4î + 5ĵ. Subtract component by component (2 − 1), then use Pythagoras, then divide each component by the length.

🎬 Exam layout, step by step
Answer: (a) r21 = 3î + 4ĵ m; (b) r21 = √(9 + 16) = 5 m; (c) r̂21 = 0.6 î + 0.8 ĵ (check: 0.36 + 0.64 = 1, so its length is 1).

5. The dot (scalar) product: E·S = ES cos θ

The dot product of two vectors gives a scalar:

A·B = AB cos θ    (θ = angle between the two arrows)

Think of it as “how much of A lies along B” (A cos θ, the projection) multiplied by B. The dot product is largest when the arrows are parallel (cos 0° = 1). It is zero when they are perpendicular (cos 90° = 0) and negative when the angle is more than 90°. In components, A·B = AxBx + AyBy + AzBz. Work done is W = F·s. In this chapter the electric flux through a small surface is Δφ = E·ΔS = E ΔS cos θ. Here ΔS is the area vector (ഏരിയ സദിശം): its length is the area and its direction is the normal (perpendicular) to the surface.

🧪 Tilt a surface in a uniform field – the dot product as flux

The surface has area ΔS = 0.05 m². At θ = 0° the surface faces the field squarely and the most lines cross it. At θ = 90° the lines skim along the surface and none cross it: the flux is zero. Beyond 90° the lines cross it “backwards”, so the flux is negative. ⚠ The angle in E ΔS cos θ is measured from the normal, not from the surface itself.

Worked example 3 · Flux through a tilted surface

Q. A uniform electric field of 2 × 10³ N/C passes through a flat surface of area 0.05 m². The normal to the surface makes 60° with the field. Find the flux E·ΔS through the surface.

Flux = E ΔS cos θ with θ = 60° (the angle with the normal). Trap: if the question says the field makes 60° with the surface, then θ = 90° − 60° = 30°.

🎬 Exam layout, step by step
Answer: φ = E ΔS cos θ = 2 × 10³ × 0.05 × 0.5 = 50 N m²/C.

6. The cross (vector) product: τ = p × E

The cross product of two vectors gives a vector:

|A × B| = AB sin θ,   direction ⟂ to both A and B (right-hand rule)

Its size equals the area of the parallelogram made by the two arrows. It is largest when they are perpendicular (sin 90° = 1) and zero when they are parallel or antiparallel (sin 0° = sin 180° = 0). For its direction, point the fingers of your right hand along A and curl them towards B; your thumb gives A × B. Order matters: B × A = −(A × B), and î × ĵ = k̂. Torque is τ = r × F (page 3). In section 1.12 the torque (ടോർക്ക് (ബലാഘൂർണം)) on an electric dipole in a field is τ = p × E, of size pE sin θ.

🧪 The cross product as an area – and the torque on a dipole

Values used: p = 2 × 10−9 C m, E = 3 × 10⁴ N/C, so pE = 6 × 10−5 N m. Watch the shaded area: it is zero at 0° and 180° and largest at 90°. The forces +qE and −qE on the two ends are equal and opposite, so the net force is zero. They still turn the dipole towards the field. ⊙ means the torque vector points out of the page (anticlockwise turning); ⊗ means into the page (clockwise).

Worked example 4 · Torque from a cross product

Q. A dipole of moment 2 × 10−9 C m makes an angle of 60° with a uniform field of 3 × 10⁴ N/C. Find the magnitude of the torque τ = p × E.

|p × E| = pE sin θ, and sin 60° ≈ 0.866. Multiply the numbers and the powers of ten separately.

🎬 Exam layout, step by step
Answer: τ = pE sin θ = 2 × 10−9 × 3 × 10⁴ × 0.866 ≈ 5.2 × 10−5 N m.
⭐ Key ideas
  • Charge and flux are scalars; force, field, dipole moment, area vector and torque are vectors.
  • Resultant: R = √(A² + B² + 2AB cos θ). Or add components: Rx = Ax + Bx, Ry = Ay + By.
  • Unit vector: Â = A/A, magnitude 1, shows direction only; î, ĵ, k̂ point along x, y, z.
  • r21 = r2 − r1 points from 1 to 2; r̂21 = r21/r21.
  • Dot product A·B = AB cos θ (a scalar; zero when perpendicular) → flux E ΔS cos θ.
  • Cross product |A × B| = AB sin θ (a vector ⟂ both; zero when parallel) → torque pE sin θ.
