Force, Displacement and Weight ബലം · സ്ഥാനാന്തരം

In physics, work is done only when a force moves an object, and only the movement in the direction of the force counts. So before we can measure work we must be sure about three things: what a force is, what displacement is, and how to compare their directions. This page also revises the forces you will meet most in the chapter – weight (the Earth's pull) and friction – and two tools from the motion chapter, F = m a and v² = u² + 2as, which you need for kinetic energy on page 8.

📖 What you need
  • Multiplying and dividing decimals, such as 0.5 × 9.8.
  • Nothing else – everything is built here.

1. Force – a push or a pull

A force (ബലം) is a push or a pull. A headload worker lifting a sack, a shopper pushing a trolley, the Earth pulling a coconut down – all are forces. A force can start an object moving, stop it, speed it up, slow it down, or change its direction. Forces are measured in newtons (N) (ന്യൂട്ടൺ). One newton is about the weight of a 100 g chocolate bar.

A force has a size and a direction, so we draw it as an arrow: the arrow points the way the force acts, and a longer arrow means a bigger force. When two forces act on the same object along one line, forces in the same direction add, and forces in opposite directions take away. What is left is the net force.

🧪 Tug of war on a box

Make the two forces equal: the net force is zero and a box at rest stays at rest, even though two people are working hard. Keep that in mind – on page 5 you will see that a force that moves nothing does no work.

2. Distance and displacement

Distance (ദൂരം) is the total length of the path you actually travel. Displacement (സ്ഥാനാന്തരം) is the straight-line change of position, from where you started to where you are now, together with its direction. Distance only grows as you move. Displacement can shrink when you turn back, and it is zero when you come back to the start.

🧪 One lap of the school track

At the school sports meet, a 400 m race is one full lap. The runner covers a distance of 400 m, but finishes exactly where she started, so her displacement is 0 m. (This track has two 100 m straights and two 100 m curves.)

⭐ Remember

In the work formula W = F × s, s is the displacement – how far the object actually moved from its starting place, along the direction of the force. A man who walks around holding a sack and comes back to the same spot has zero displacement.

3. Comparing the direction of a force with the direction of motion

This chapter keeps asking one question: is the object moving in the direction of the force (ബലത്തിന്റെ ദിശ)? There are three simple cases:

  • Along the motion – the force points the same way the object moves (a push from behind). It helps the motion.
  • Opposite to the motion – the force points the other way (friction, brakes). It fights the motion.
  • Perpendicular to the motion – the force is at a right angle (90°) to the motion, like gravity on a trolley rolling along a level floor. It neither helps nor fights the motion along the floor.
🧪 Which way does the force point?

The trolley moves to the right (green arrow = displacement). Pick each force in turn and look at the angle between the two arrows: 0° means along, 180° means opposite, 90° means perpendicular.

🤔 Look ahead

On page 6 these three cases become positive work (പോസിറ്റീവ് പ്രവൃത്തി, force along the motion), negative work (നെഗറ്റീവ് പ്രവൃത്തി, force opposite to the motion) and no work (force perpendicular to the motion).

4. Weight: W = m g

The mass (മാസ്) of an object is the amount of matter in it, in kilograms (kg). Its weight (ഭാരം) is the force with which the Earth pulls it down – the force of gravity (ഗുരുത്വാകർഷണബലം / ഭൂഗുരുത്വാകർഷണബലം). Near the Earth's surface every kilogram is pulled with about 9.8 N, so

weight = m × g    (m in kg, g = 9.8 m/s², weight in N)

Questions often say “take g = 10 m/s²” to make the arithmetic easy – always use the value the question gives. On the Moon g is only about 1.6 m/s².

🧪 How hard must the crane pull?

To lift a load steadily (at a constant speed), the upward pull must be equal to the weight, m g. This is the force you will multiply by the height on page 6 to get the work done against gravity, W = m g h.

⚠ Never stand under a load being lifted by a crane. When you lift something heavy yourself, bend your knees and keep your back straight.

Worked example 1 · A sack of rice

Q. A headload worker lifts a 50 kg sack of rice steadily onto a lorry. What upward force must he apply? (g = 10 m/s²)

Lifting steadily means the upward force equals the weight. Weight = m × g.

🎬 Exam layout, step by step
Answer: F = m g = 50 kg × 10 m/s² = 500 N, upwards.

5. Friction – the force that opposes motion

Friction (ഘർഷണബലം) acts where two surfaces touch, and it always opposes the sliding or rolling. A heavier load presses harder on the floor, so the friction is bigger. To keep a wheelchair moving at a steady speed on a level corridor, the attendant must push with a force equal to the friction: then the net force is zero and the speed does not change. Push harder and it speeds up; push less and it slows down.

🧪 Pushing a wheelchair along a hospital corridor

The wheelchair starts at 1 m/s. With a 40 N push for patient 1 (friction 40 N), the speed stays at 1 m/s. For patient 2 you need 70 N for the same steady speed. These are the numbers in the textbook's wheelchair example (page 5 of this guide).

6. Net force and acceleration: F = m a

When the forces do not balance, the object's velocity (പ്രവേഗം) changes – it has an acceleration (ത്വരണം), the change in velocity every second, in m/s². Newton's second law links them:

net force F = m × a    (F in N, m in kg, a in m/s²)

So 1 N is the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s². The same force gives a heavy object a smaller acceleration than a light one.

🧪 Same push, different trolleys

The trolley starts from rest. Each bar is its velocity after one more second: it grows by a = F ÷ m every second. Double the mass and the bars grow only half as fast.

Worked example 2 · A supermarket trolley

Q. A loaded supermarket trolley of mass 20 kg speeds up with an acceleration of 0.5 m/s². What net force acts on it?

Use F = m × a with m in kg and a in m/s².

🎬 Exam layout, step by step
Answer: F = m a = 20 kg × 0.5 m/s² = 10 N.

7. Speed after a fall: v² = u² + 2as

From the motion chapter you know an equation that links the initial velocity u, the final velocity v, the acceleration a and the displacement s, without needing the time:

v² = u² + 2 a s

For an object that falls from rest, u = 0, the acceleration is g and the displacement is the height h fallen, so v² = 2 g h. And if u = 0, the general equation gives v² = 2as, that is a s = v² ÷ 2. On page 8 this little step turns the work formula W = F s = (m a) s into the kinetic energy formula Ek = ½ m v².

🧪 Drop a coconut

Air resistance is ignored. Notice that four times the height gives only twice the speed (5 m → 10 m/s, 20 m → 20 m/s with g = 10), because the height decides v², not v.

⚠ Never stand under a coconut tree with ripe coconuts, especially on a windy day – a coconut falling 20 m hits the ground at about 70 km/h.

Worked example 3 · A falling coconut

Q. A coconut falls from rest from the top of a 20 m tall coconut tree. Find its velocity just before it hits the ground. (g = 10 m/s², ignore air resistance)

Falls from rest → u = 0. Use v² = u² + 2 a s with a = g and s = h. Find v², then take the square root.

🎬 Exam layout, step by step
Answer: v² = 0 + 2 × 10 × 20 = 400, so v = √400 = 20 m/s (about 72 km/h).
