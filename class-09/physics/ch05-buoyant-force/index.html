Buoyant Force പ്ലവക്ഷമബലം

The chapter opens with INS Vikrant, the giant aircraft carrier built at Cochin Shipyard, sailing past the Chinese fishing nets of Kochi. A boy wonders: “Even a very light iron nail sinks in water. So how does a ship that weighs thousands of tonnes float on the sea?” By the end of this chapter you will answer him. You will discover the upward push that every liquid and gas gives to things in it (the buoyant force) and learn to measure it with a spring balance. You will find out what makes it bigger, and meet Archimedes' principle and the principle of floatation. You will also see how a lactometer at the milk society catches milk mixed with water. This interactive, animated self-study guide starts from the basics – mass and weight, volume, density and pressure. It explains every idea with Kerala examples, answers every textbook question, and ends with a revision sheet and a model test.

The chapter in one picture

🚢 A ship takes on cargo · an iron nail sinks

Left: the sea pushes the ship up with the buoyant force (പ്ലവക്ഷമബലം, green arrow). As containers are loaded, the ship's weight (ഭാരം, red arrow) grows, and the ship sinks a little deeper. The deeper it sits, the more water it pushes aside, and the bigger the buoyant force becomes. It settles where the buoyant force is exactly equal to its weight – the two arrows are always the same length. This is the principle of floatation (പ്ലവനതത്വം, page 8). Right: a 5 g iron nail can push aside only a tiny amount of water (less than 1 cm³), so the buoyant force on it is only about one-eighth of its weight. It sinks (മുങ്ങുക). The ship is made of iron too, but its hollow shape pushes aside a huge volume of water. (Numbers: a box-shaped hull 200 m × 30 m in sea water of density ≈ 1025 kg/m³; the vertical scale of the ship is stretched so that you can see the change.)

⚠ Never lean over a well or play near deep water. Do the water experiments of this chapter in a bucket or tub, with an adult nearby.
