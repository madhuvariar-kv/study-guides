Density – Mass per Volume സാന്ദ്രത

Why does a huge teak log float down the Periyar while a tiny iron nail sinks? Why does coconut oil sit on top of water? The answer is not “heavy or light” but density: how much mass is packed into each bit of volume. The chapter uses density everywhere. It decides the buoyant force, whether things float, and what a hydrometer and a lactometer measure. This page builds the idea from mass (page 1) and volume (page 2).

📖 What you need
  • Mass in g and kg (page 1); volume in cm³ and m³ (page 2).
  • Dividing decimals and powers of ten.

1. Same size, different mass

Take a wooden ball and an iron ball of exactly the same size. They have the same volume, yet the iron ball is far heavier: in the same space, iron packs much more mass. To compare materials fairly, we ask: how much mass is there in one unit of volume? That is the density (സാന്ദ്രത):

density ρ = mass mvolume V   ·   m = ρ × V   ·   V = mρ

The symbol ρ is the Greek letter “rho”. Density is a property of the material, not of the object. A small iron nail and a big iron rod have the same density, 7800 kg/m³, because if you cut a piece of 1 cm³ from either, it has the same mass.

🧪 Density machine

Each dot is 10 g of mass, and the size of the square shows the volume. More dots in the same square means a denser material. Keep the mass fixed and make the volume bigger: the dots spread out and the density falls. Try 540 g in 200 cm³ (aluminium) and 100 g in 100 cm³ (water).

2. Units of density

Density is mass ÷ volume, so its unit is a mass unit ÷ a volume unit. In SI units it is kilogram per cubic metre, kg/m³; in the laboratory we often use gram per cubic centimetre, g/cm³. To convert, remember 1 g = 10⁻³ kg and 1 cm³ = 10⁻⁶ m³:

1 g/cm³ = 10⁻³ kg10⁻⁶ m³ = 1000 kg/m³   ·   g/cm³ → kg/m³: × 1000   ·   kg/m³ → g/cm³: ÷ 1000

Water has a density of 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³: every cubic centimetre of water has a mass of 1 g, every litre 1 kg, and every cubic metre 1000 kg (a tonne).

Worked example 1 · What metal is it?

Q. A metal block has a mass of 540 g and a volume of 200 cm³. Find its density in g/cm³ and in kg/m³. Which metal could it be? (Use the table in section 3.)

ρ = m ÷ V = 540 ÷ 200. Multiply by 1000 to change g/cm³ into kg/m³, then look for that value in the table.

🎬 Exam layout, step by step
Answer: ρ = 540 g ÷ 200 cm³ = 2.7 g/cm³ = 2700 kg/m³ – aluminium.

⭐ Density

Density = mass ÷ volume, ρ = m/V. SI unit kg/m³; 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³. Water: 1000 kg/m³. Density belongs to the material: every piece of iron has the same density.

4. Same mass, different volume

Turn the formula around: V = m ÷ ρ. For the same mass, a denser material takes up a smaller volume. One kilogram of copper is a smaller block than one kilogram of iron, and one kilogram of teak is a big block. You will need exactly this idea on page 6, where the textbook compares an iron block and a copper block of equal weight (Table 5.4).

🧪 One kilogram of each

All five blocks balance each other on a common balance – same mass – but look at the sizes! The volumes are worked out as V = m ÷ ρ, and the cubes are drawn to the same scale.

Worked example 2 · Volume of 1 kg of iron

Q. Iron has a density of 7800 kg/m³. Find the volume of a 1 kg iron block, in m³ and in cm³.

V = m ÷ ρ = 1 ÷ 7800 m³. To change m³ into cm³, multiply by 10⁶.

🎬 Exam layout, step by step
Answer: V = 1 kg ÷ 7800 kg/m³ = 0.000 128 2 m³ ≈ 1.282 × 10⁻⁴ m³ ≈ 128.2 cm³. (A 1 kg copper block is only about 112.4 cm³.)

5. Heavy is not the same as dense

A teak log on a timber lorry may weigh a tonne, while an iron nail weighs a few grams. The log is far heavier, but iron is far denser. Whether something floats in water depends on its density, not on its weight:

  • Density of the object less than the liquid's → it floats (പൊങ്ങിക്കിടക്കുക), with part of it above the surface.
  • Density of the object more than the liquid's → it sinks (മുങ്ങുക).

You will see why on pages 4–8. For now, test the rule. When an object floats, the fraction of it under the surface turns out to be ρobject ÷ ρliquid (page 8 explains this).

🧪 Float or sink? Compare the densities

Ice in water: 917 ÷ 1000 ≈ 0.92, so about nine-tenths of an iceberg is hidden under the sea. Try iron in mercury – iron floats! Wax sinks in kerosene but floats in water. Densities are in kg/m³.

6. Liquids on liquids: oil on water

The same rule works for liquids that do not mix. Coconut oil (920 kg/m³) is less dense than water (1000 kg/m³), so oil poured into water rises and spreads as a layer on top. Kerosene does the same. When you wash a greasy vessel, the oil drops float to the top of the water.

🌍 Kerala examples

Teak and bamboo logs were once floated down rivers like the Periyar and the Chaliyar to the timber markets: wood is less dense than water. A coconut falling into the backwaters floats away, and can grow on another beach. An oil spill from a boat spreads as a shiny film on top of Kochi harbour water. And at the milk society, the lactometer checks the density of milk to catch added water (page 9).
