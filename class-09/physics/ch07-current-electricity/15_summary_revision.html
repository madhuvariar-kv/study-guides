2. The key boxes of the textbook

1Capacitor (p. 132)കപ്പാസിറ്റർ

Stores electrical energy and, when needed, supplies a current for a short time by discharging. Used in almost all electronic devices. A cell, instead, keeps a current going continuously. – page 5

2Electric current (p. 132)വൈദ്യുതപ്രവാഹം

The flow of charges through a circuit creates an electric current. – page 5

3Potential difference (p. 134)പൊട്ടൻഷ്യൽ വ്യത്യാസം · ഇലക്ട്രിക് പൊട്ടൻഷ്യൽ

The work done to move a unit charge from one point to another in a circuit. Electric potential = the potential energy of a unit charge at a point. – page 5

4Source of electricity (p. 134)വൈദ്യുതസ്രോതസ്സ്

A source used to maintain a potential difference between two points of a circuit – like a pump keeping a water-level difference. – page 5

5emf and the volt (p. 135)വിദ്യുത്ചാലകബലം (emf) · വോൾട്ട് (V)
V = W ÷ Q

emf: the potential difference between the terminals of a source when no current is drawn – the most it can give. Unit J/C = volt; 1 V = 1 J of work per coulomb. – page 5

6Alessandro Volta (p. 136)വോൾട്ട്

Italy, 1745–1827. Invented the electric cell (voltaic pile); discovered methane; professor at Pavia. The volt is named after him. – page 6

7Primary and secondary cells (p. 136–137)പ്രൈമറി സെൽ / സെക്കൻഡറി സെൽ · സംഭരണ സെല്ലുകൾ

Primary cells cannot be reused (dry cell, button cell); secondary (storage) cells can be recharged (mobile, car, inverter battery). A battery = several cells working as one source. – page 6

8Cells in series (p. 138)ശ്രേണീരീതി
emf = e1 + e2 + …

+ of one cell to − of the next: the emfs add. Two 1.5 V cells → 3 V; four → 6 V. – page 6

9Cells in parallel (p. 139)സമാന്തരരീതി

Identical cells + to +, − to −: the same emf as one cell, but the current lasts longer (power banks, mobile phones). Two cells joined + to + give 0 V – never do it. – page 6

10Electric current and the ampere (p. 140)വൈദ്യുതപ്രവാഹതീവ്രത · ആമ്പിയർ (A)
I = Q ÷ t

The charge flowing per unit time. Unit C/s = ampere, after André-Marie Ampère (France, 1775–1836, began electromagnetism). 1 A = 1000 mA; 1 mA = 1000 µA. – page 7

11Direction of current (p. 141)ഇലക്ട്രോൺ · കറന്റ്

Electrons move from the − to the + terminal in the wire, but the (conventional) current is taken from + to −. A clamp ammeter measures large currents without being connected into the circuit. – page 7

12Electricity in living things (p. 142)ജീവികളിലും വൈദ്യുതി

The brain, nerves and heart work with electric signals; the sinus node sets the heartbeat. The electric eel can produce about 600 V to catch its prey. – page 7

13Resistance and Ohm’s law (p. 144)പ്രതിരോധം · ഓം നിയമം
V = IR  ·  R = V ÷ I

Resistance opposes the flow of current. Ohm’s law: at constant temperature the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it. Georg Simon Ohm, 1789–1854. – page 8

14The ohm (p. 145)ഓം (Ω)

Unit V/A = ohm (Ω); 1 kΩ = 103 Ω, 1 MΩ = 106 Ω. Resistance is caused by collisions of free electrons with the atoms of the conductor. – page 8

15Resistors and the multimeter (p. 146)പ്രതിരോധകം · മൾട്ടിമീറ്റർ

A resistor puts a definite resistance into a circuit (colour codes show its value). A multimeter measures resistance, potential difference and current. – page 9

16What affects resistance (p. 148)ഛേദതല പരപ്പളവ് (വണ്ണം) · നിക്രോം · റിയോസ്റ്റാറ്റ്

Material, length (longer → more), area of cross-section (thicker → less) and temperature. Nichrome high, copper low, silver lowest. Superconductors: zero resistance at very low temperature. A rheostat changes R by changing the length of wire in the circuit. – page 9

17Series combination (p. 150)സഫലപ്രതിരോധം · ശ്രേണീരീതി
R = R1 + R2  ·  n equal: nR

Effective resistance = the single resistance with the same effect. In series the current is the same and V = V1 + V2. – page 10

18Parallel combination (p. 151–152)സമാന്തരരീതി
1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2  ·  R = R1R2 ÷ (R1 + R2)

Same potential difference; currents add I = I1 + I2; n equal resistors: R/n. The effective resistance decreases. – page 10

19DC and AC (p. 153)ദിഷ്ടധാരാവൈദ്യുതി (DC) / പ്രത്യാവർത്തിധാരാവൈദ്യുതി (AC)

A battery gives current in one direction – direct current. The household supply changes direction at regular intervals – alternating current – at a high voltage, 230 V. Never do these experiments with household electricity. – page 10

⚠️ Common mistakes
  • Connecting a voltmeter in series or an ammeter in parallel, or ignoring the + and − terminals. Voltmeter across, ammeter in the loop, + towards the + of the cell.
  • Thinking current is “used up” in a series circuit. The same current flows through every part; it is the potential difference that is shared.
  • Adding resistances in parallel. Use 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2: 6 Ω ∥ 3 Ω = 2 Ω, less than the smallest.
  • Forgetting to convert mA → A or kΩ → Ω. 20 mA = 0.02 A; 2 kΩ = 2000 Ω.
  • Mixing up electron flow (− to +) with conventional current (+ to −).
  • Saying the emf of cells in parallel adds up. Identical cells in parallel give the emf of one cell.
  • Writing the unit of resistance as “Ω/A”, or using R = V/I with V not in volts and I not in amperes. Ω = V/A.
  • Experimenting with household (mains) electricity – never.
⚠ Never experiment with the mains supply (230 V AC) at home – it can kill. Use only torch cells or a 9 V battery.
