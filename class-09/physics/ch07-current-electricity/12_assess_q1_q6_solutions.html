Question 1 · Chemical energy → electrical energy

1. താഴെ കൊടുത്തവയിൽ രാസോർജത്തെ വൈദ്യുതോർജമാക്കുന്നത് ഏത്? Which of the following changes chemical energy രാസോർജം into electrical energy വൈദ്യുതോർജം?

a) dry cell ഡ്രൈസെൽb) dynamo ഡൈനാമോc) solar cell സോളാർ സെൽ

Ask of each one: what goes in? A dynamo needs something to turn it; a solar cell needs light; a dry cell needs nothing but the chemicals inside it.

🎬 Solution animation – what goes in, what comes out
(a) dry cell. Inside a dry cell (torch cell) a chemical reaction pushes electrons to the − terminal, so chemical energy → electrical energy (Table 7.2).
(b) a dynamo changes mechanical energy (the turning wheel) into electrical energy; (c) a solar cell changes light energy into electrical energy.
⚠️ Common trap

All three give electricity, so all three “look right”. The question asks about the input energy. Only the cell starts from chemical energy – that is why a dry cell is used up when its chemicals are spent.

Question 2 · What must the two terminals have?

2. ഒരു അടഞ്ഞ സെർക്കീട്ടിൽ വൈദ്യുതപ്രവാഹം ഉണ്ടാകണമെങ്കിൽ അതിലെ സെല്ലിന്റെ രണ്ട് ടെർമിനലുകൾ For an electric current to flow in a closed circuit, the two terminals ടെർമിനലുകൾ of the cell in it must

a) be at a high potential ഉയർന്ന പൊട്ടെൻഷ്യലിൽ ആയിരിക്കണംb) have a potential difference between them തമ്മിൽ ഒരു പൊട്ടെൻഷ്യൽ വ്യത്യാസം ഉണ്ടായിരിക്കണംc) be at different temperatures വ്യത്യസ്ത താപനിലയിൽ ആയിരിക്കണംd) be at different heights വ്യത്യസ്ത ഉയരത്തിൽ ആയിരിക്കണം

Remember the two water pots of Fig 7.3 and 7.4: water flowed only when the levels were different. What plays the role of the “level” for electric charges?

🎬 Solution animation – flow needs a difference
🧪 Try it – set the potential of each terminal

Model: the bulb is treated as a fixed resistance of 6 Ω, so I = (difference in potential) ÷ 6 Ω. Put both terminals at 9 V – both are at a “high potential”, yet nothing flows. That rules out option (a).

(b) have a potential difference between them. Charges flow from higher to lower potential only when there is a difference of potential between the two ends of the circuit, just as water flows only when there is a difference in level. The cell keeps this difference going, so the current is continuous.
(a) is wrong: two terminals at the same high potential have zero difference – no current. (c) a temperature difference makes heat flow; (d) a height difference makes water flow (Table 7.1) – not electric charge.

Question 3 · Fig 7.34 – which circuit has both meters right?

3. താഴെക്കൊടുത്തവയിൽ അമ്മീറ്ററും വോൾട്ട്മീറ്ററും ശരിയായ രീതിയിൽ ബന്ധിപ്പിച്ച സെർക്കീട്ട് ഏത്? In which of the circuits below (Fig 7.34) are the ammeter അമ്മീറ്റർ and the voltmeter വോൾട്ട്മീറ്റർ connected correctly?

Check four things in each circuit: (1) is the ammeter in series? (2) is the voltmeter in parallel with the bulb? (3) and (4) does the current enter each meter at its + terminal? First find which side of the battery is + (the long line).

Model (Fig 7.34 gives no values): battery of two 1.5 V cells = 3 V; the bulb is a fixed 6 Ω; ideal ammeter (almost 0 Ω); voltmeter with a very large resistance.

CircuitAmmeter positionAmmeter + terminalVoltmeter positionVoltmeter + terminalVerdict
(a)in series ✔current enters at + ✔in series ✘(enters at +)✘
(b)across the bulb ✘–in series ✘–✘

Circuit (c). The battery's + terminal is the long line on the right, so the current leaves it towards the switch, goes up through the ammeter, right-to-left through the bulb and back to −.
In (c) the ammeter is in series and the current enters it at its + terminal (the + is on the side joined to the cell's +); the voltmeter is in parallel with the bulb, with its + on the right-hand end of the bulb, which is at the higher potential. Both are correct.
(a) the voltmeter is in series ✘. (b) the ammeter is across the bulb and the voltmeter is in series ✘. (d) both meters are in the right places, but both are connected the wrong way round (+ and − reversed) ✘.
⚠️ Common trap

Looking only at where the meters are and ignoring the + and − marks – that makes (d) look correct. An analogue meter connected the wrong way round tries to swing backwards and hits its stop (it can be damaged); a digital meter shows a minus sign.

💡 Why this matters

An ammeter has almost no resistance – put it across a component (as in (b)) and it becomes a short circuit. A voltmeter has a very large resistance – put it in series (as in (a)) and it almost stops the current, so the bulb goes out. This is the most common practical-exam mistake.

Question 5 · 50 J to carry 5 C from M to N

5. ഒരു വൈദ്യുത സെർക്കീട്ടിലെ M എന്ന ബിന്ദുവിൽ നിന്ന് 5 C വൈദ്യുത ചാർജ് N എന്ന ബിന്ദുവിൽ എത്തിക്കാൻ 50 J പ്രവൃത്തി ചെയ്തുവെങ്കിൽ M, N എന്നീ ബിന്ദുക്കൾക്കിടയിലെ പൊട്ടെൻഷ്യൽ വ്യത്യാസം എത്ര? If 50 J of work is done to carry an electric charge of 5 C from a point M to a point N in an electric circuit, what is the potential difference between M and N?

Potential difference = work done per unit charge. Which number goes on top?

🎬 Solution in exam layout – Given → Formula → Substitution → Answer
🧪 Try other numbers
Given: work W = 50 J; charge Q = 5 C.
Formula: V = W/Q.
Substitution: V = 50 J ÷ 5 C.
Answer: V = 10 V (10 J of work for every coulomb).
⚠️ Common trap

Writing Q/W = 5/50 = 0.1. Check the unit: the volt is joule per coulomb (J/C), so work goes on top. 1 V means 1 J for each 1 C.

Question 6 · Fig 7.35 – which bulbs are in series?

💡 Quick test

Imagine unscrewing one bulb. In a series circuit the other bulb goes out too (the only path is broken). In a parallel circuit the other bulb stays lit – that is why the lights and fans at home are in parallel.
