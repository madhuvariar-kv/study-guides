Applications & Explore Online ശബ്ദം

A doctor listens to your heart through a stethoscope, a ship finds the depth of the Arabian Sea with echoes, a scanner shows a baby before it is born, a wall and a row of trees keep a hospital quiet, soft curtains make an auditorium clear, and counting the seconds after a flash tells you how far away the storm is. Every lab below uses only the ideas of this chapter – and the computer works out every number from distance = speed × time. All labs are silent: you see the sound, you do not hear it.

📖 The ideas behind every lab
  • Sound is produced by vibration (കമ്പനം) and travels as compressions and rarefactions – page 5, page 3.
  • Sound needs a medium (മാധ്യമം) – a solid, a liquid or a gas; it cannot travel through vacuum (ശൂന്യത) – page 6.
  • The speed of sound (ശബ്ദവേഗം) depends on the medium (Table 8.3: air 347 m/s, sea water 1525 m/s, steel 5944 m/s at 25 °C); distance = speed × time – page 7.
  • To hear we need a source, a medium and an organ of hearing; loud sound and noise pollution (ശബ്ദമലിനീകരണം) harm us – page 8, page 9.

Labs marked go further are beyond the textbook – enjoy them, but your exam stays with the textbook ideas. Values marked “approx.” (decibels, sound in body tissue) are typical values, not from the textbook.

1. The doctor’s stethoscope

A stethoscope (സ്റ്റെതസ്കോപ്പ്) has a chest piece with a thin diaphragm, a rubber tube and two earpieces. Each beat of the heart (“lub … dub”) makes the chest wall and the diaphragm vibrate. The vibrations travel as compressions and rarefactions through the air inside the tube straight to the doctor’s ears. In open air the same faint sound would spread out in every direction and become too weak to hear; the tube keeps it together, so it reaches the ear strong and clear. Nothing is made louder by electricity – it is only the medium guiding the sound.

🩺 Stethoscope lab: follow the heartbeat to the doctor’s ears

The pulses are drawn slowed down. In reality the sound crosses the 60 cm tube in about 0.0017 s (0.6 m ÷ 347 m/s). The loudness bar is only a comparison, not a measurement.

📝 In the exam this looks like: “Through which medium does the sound of the heartbeat reach the doctor’s ears in a stethoscope?” → mainly through the air in the tube (after making the diaphragm vibrate). “Why can the doctor hear the heartbeat clearly?” → the sound is guided along the tube instead of spreading out in all directions, so it reaches the ear without becoming weak. Sound needs a medium – here the medium is the air in the tube.

2. SONAR and echoes – how deep is the sea? go further

When sound meets a hard surface it bounces back – an echo. Ships use this in SONAR (SOund Navigation And Ranging): a device under the hull sends a short pulse of sound down into the sea and times how long the echo from the sea bed takes to come back. The sound goes down and back up, so it travels twice the depth: depth = speed × time ÷ 2, using the speed of sound in sea water, 1525 m/s (Table 8.3). Dolphins do the same with clicks to find fish in murky water – this is called echolocation; bats do it in air.

📡 SONAR lab: send a ping, time the echo

The ship’s ping is shown in real time; the dolphin’s clicks are shown 20 times slower. The sea bed is an example shape: a shallow shelf near the coast, a steep slope and an undersea hill. Speed of sound in sea water: 1525 m/s (Table 8.3).

📝 In the exam this looks like: “A ship sends a sound pulse and receives the echo from the sea bed 2 s later. The speed of sound in sea water is 1525 m/s. Find the depth.” → the sound goes down and comes back, so depth = 1525 × 2 ÷ 2 = 1525 m. “Why can dolphins and whales talk to each other far apart?” → sound travels well through water (a liquid medium), faster than in air (1525 m/s against 347 m/s) – textbook Fig 8.11.

3. Ultrasound scanning go further

We can hear sounds whose vibrations are between about 20 and 20 000 per second (20 Hz – 20 kHz). Faster vibrations are ultrasound – too high-pitched for us to hear (dogs and bats hear some of it). A scanning machine’s probe sends very short ultrasound pulses into the body. Each time a pulse meets the boundary of an organ, a little of it comes back as an echo. From the echo times (sound travels about 1540 m/s in soft body tissue, approx.) the computer works out the depth of each boundary and builds a picture. Ultrasound scans are safe – no X-rays – so doctors use them to check a baby before birth, the kidneys, the liver and the heart. A jelly is spread on the skin first, because a layer of air would reflect almost all the ultrasound before it entered the body.

🩻 Scanner lab: slide the probe and build the picture

A simplified model: every boundary sends back an echo, and its depth = speed × echo time ÷ 2 with 1540 m/s (approx.). Real scanners send thousands of pulses every second and use far more probe positions.

📝 In the exam this looks like: “Name a use of sound in medicine.” → a doctor’s stethoscope; ultrasound scanning to see organs or a baby before birth. “Sound is a form of energy – give a use of sound in daily life” (textbook p157) → talking, music, alarms and bells, horns, a doctor’s stethoscope, scanning.

4. Noise barriers and silence zones

Sound gets weaker as it spreads out: for a single source in open air, every time you double the distance the sound level drops by about 6 dB (decibel, the unit of sound level – approx. values). A solid wall between the road and a building blocks the direct path and cuts about 10 dB (approx.); a thick belt of trees helps a little more. That is why the textbook says to plant trees, use box-type loudspeakers instead of horn-type ones, ban air horns, and keep the areas around hospitals, schools and courts as silence zones. In India, the noise rules (2000) set a silence zone of at least 100 m around them, with limits of about 50 dB by day and 40 dB at night; loudspeakers are not allowed in public places between 10 pm and 6 am.

🏥 Noise lab: keep the hospital quiet

Model: level at the building = level at 10 m − 20 × log₁₀(distance ÷ 10 m) − barrier. This “−6 dB for each doubling” rule is for a single source in open air; real streets, buildings and wind change it (approx.). Limits: silence zone 50 dB by day / 40 dB at night; residential area 55 / 45 dB.

⚠ Sound above about 85 dB for a long time damages hearing. Stay far from loudspeakers and firecrackers at festivals (Thrissur Pooram, Vishu), and never put anything into your ears to “block” the sound – use proper ear plugs or ear muffs.

📝 In the exam this looks like: Let us assess Q8 – “Write two ways to reduce noise pollution.” → avoid air horns and keep silencers working; use box-type loudspeakers instead of horn-type; no loudspeakers before 6 am and after 10 pm; plant trees; keep silence near hospitals, schools and courts; ear plugs and ear muffs for workers near loud machines. “Who are affected most by noise?” → traffic police, factory and construction workers, patients in hospitals, babies, old people, students.

5. Auditoriums and concert halls

In a hall, the sound from the stage reaches you directly and also after bouncing off the walls, the ceiling and the floor. Hard, smooth surfaces – concrete, plaster, marble, glass – reflect almost all the sound, so it keeps bouncing and lingers: words overlap and become hard to follow, like in a big empty hall. Soft, porous materials – curtains, carpets, cushioned seats, soft boards, acoustic ceiling tiles, and even the clothes of the audience – absorb sound: their fibres take up the energy of the vibrations. Good halls mix both, so that speech is clear and music still sounds full. (Sound engineers like Resul Pookutty control this in film studios.)

🎭 Hall lab: clap on the stage and watch the reflections

Example hall: 30 m long, 20 m wide, 10 m high (volume 6000 m³; walls 1000 m², ceiling 600 m², floor 600 m²). Each surface absorbs a share of the sound that hits it (absorption values approx.). The time for the sound to die away to one-millionth of its energy (−60 dB) is found with Sabine’s formula T = 0.161 × volume ÷ total absorption – a go-further idea. The cross-section is drawn with heights 1.5 times taller.

📝 In the exam this looks like: “Why are the walls of an auditorium covered with curtains or soft boards?” → soft, porous materials absorb sound, so less of it is reflected; the sound does not keep bouncing around (no confusing echoes) and speech is heard clearly. “Why does your voice sound different in an empty room than in a furnished one?” → in the empty room the hard walls and floor reflect the sound many times.

6. How far is the thunderstorm? Lightning safety in the monsoon

Lightning and thunder happen at the same moment. The flash reaches you almost at once (light: 3 × 108 m/s), but the thunder travels at only about 350 m/s. So count the seconds from the flash to the thunder: distance = 350 × seconds. Every 3 seconds is about 1 km. In Kerala, thunderstorms are common on pre-monsoon afternoons (April–May) and in the thulavarsham season (October–November). If you can hear thunder, lightning can reach you – go indoors.

⚡ Storm lab: flash, count, find the distance

Sound at 350 m/s, as in the textbook’s examples (347 m/s in Table 8.3 at 25 °C). The animation runs in real time up to 8 s; for longer gaps it is speeded up, but the timer still shows the real seconds.

⚠ When thunder roars, go indoors – into a building or a closed car or bus – and stay in for 30 minutes after the last thunder. Keep away from tall trees, open grounds, terraces, ponds, rivers and the sea, and metal fences. Do not use wired phones or touch electrical appliances; unplug the TV. If you are caught in the open, crouch low on your feet with your feet together – do not lie flat and do not shelter under a lone tree.

📝 In the exam this looks like: “A flash of lightning is seen and the thunder is heard 5 s later. How far away is the lightning? (speed of sound 350 m/s)” → distance = speed × time = 350 × 5 = 1750 m. “Why do we see the lightning before we hear the thunder?” → light (3 × 108 m/s) is far faster than sound (about 350 m/s). Same as the textbook’s firecracker example: 350 × 3 = 1050 m.
