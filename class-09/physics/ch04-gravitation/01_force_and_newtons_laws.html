Force and Newton's Laws ബലം · ചലനനിയമങ്ങൾ

Gravitation is a force. To understand it we first need to be clear about what a force is, how we measure it, and what it does to things that move. This page starts from zero: pushes and pulls, forces you can feel without touching, the unit newton, balanced and unbalanced forces, and Isaac Newton's three laws of motion. The chapter uses all of these.

📖 What you need
  • Nothing from earlier classes is assumed – every idea is explained here.
  • Speed and acceleration are used lightly; they are explained fully on page 4.

1. What is a force?

A force (ബലം) is a push or a pull. You push a stalled autorickshaw to start it, pull a bucket of water up from the well, and squeeze a ball of rice. In each case your force does something to the object. A force can:

  • make a resting object start moving, or make a moving object speed up;
  • slow down or stop a moving object;
  • change the direction of motion;
  • change the shape of an object.
🎬 Animation: four things a force can do
🌍 Real life in Kerala

In a game of football on the school ground a kick starts the ball, the goalkeeper's hands stop it, a header changes its direction, and a hard kick even flattens it for a moment. Four effects, one football, all caused by forces.

2. Contact and non-contact forces

Most forces need touching: your hand on a door, the rope on a bucket, the road's friction on a tyre. These are contact forces. A few forces act across empty space, with nothing touching: a magnet pulls an iron pin from a distance, a rubbed comb pulls bits of paper, and the Earth pulls a coconut down from the top of a palm. These are non-contact forces.

✋ Contact forces

  • pushing a cart, pulling a rope
  • friction between tyre and road
  • a bat hitting a ball
  • the floor holding you up

🧲 Non-contact forces

  • magnetic force (magnet and pin)
  • electric force (rubbed comb and paper)
  • gravitational force – the Earth pulling everything (ഭൂഗുരുത്വാകർഷണബലം)
⭐ Key idea

Gravity is a non-contact, attractive force (ആകർഷണബലം). Nothing touches a falling mango, yet the Earth pulls it down. This whole chapter is about this pull.

3. Forces are arrows; the unit is the newton

A force has a size and a direction. “Push with 50 N” is not enough – push which way? So we draw every force as an arrow: the arrow points in the direction of the force, and its length shows its size (drawn to a scale, e.g. 1 cm for 100 N).

The SI unit of force is the newton (N), after Isaac Newton. 1 N is the force that gives a mass of 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s² (you will see why in section 5). For a feel: the Earth pulls a 100 g packet of biscuits with about 1 N.

When several forces act on one object, what matters is their total, the net force. If the forces cancel, they are balanced (സന്തുലിതബലങ്ങൾ). If they do not cancel, they are unbalanced (അസന്തുലിതബലങ്ങൾ), and the object's motion changes in the direction of the net force.

🧪 Tug of war: balanced or unbalanced?

Make the two pulls equal: the arrows cancel and the rope does not start to move (balanced). Make them different: the net force (purple arrow) points towards the stronger team, and the rope starts moving that way, faster and faster.

🌍 Real life

In the Onam tug of war (വടംവലി), two teams can strain with all their might while the rope stays still. Huge forces, but balanced, so there is no change in motion. The moment one team tires, the forces become unbalanced and the rope starts to move.

4. Newton's first law: things keep doing what they are doing

A book on a table stays there until something pushes it. A ball rolling on smooth ice keeps rolling for a long time. Newton put this in his first law of motion: an object stays at rest, or keeps moving in a straight line at the same speed, unless an unbalanced force acts on it. This “unwillingness to change” is called inertia (ജഡത്വം). The more mass an object has, the more inertia it has.

🎬 Animation: the coin, the card and the tumbler
🌍 Real life: the KSRTC bus

When a bus brakes suddenly, the bus slows down but your body keeps moving forward, so you lurch towards the front – inertia of motion. When a stopped bus starts with a jerk, your feet move with the floor but your upper body stays behind, so you fall backwards – inertia of rest. That is why you hold the rail!

5. Newton's second law: F = ma

An unbalanced force changes velocity; in other words it produces an acceleration (ത്വരണം), the change in velocity every second (page 4). Newton's second law of motion (രണ്ടാം ചലനനിയമം) tells us exactly how much:

F = m × a    (force in N, mass in kg, acceleration in m/s²)

Read it two ways: a bigger force on the same mass gives a bigger acceleration; the same force on a bigger mass gives a smaller acceleration (a = F/m). And 1 N = 1 kg × 1 m/s², which is how the newton is defined.

🧪 Same force, different masses

Friction is ignored. The figure shows where each cart is after 2 s of the same push. Double the mass of a cart and its acceleration halves; double the force and both accelerations double.

Worked example 1 · The handcart

Q. A vegetable seller pushes a loaded handcart of total mass 20 kg with an unbalanced force of 60 N. What is the acceleration of the cart?

You know F and m. Rearrange F = ma to get a = F/m.

🎬 Exam layout, step by step
Answer: a = F/m = 60 N ÷ 20 kg = 3 m/s². The cart's velocity grows by 3 m/s every second.

Worked example 2 · The KSRTC bus

Q. A KSRTC bus of mass 10 000 kg speeds up with an acceleration of 1.5 m/s². What unbalanced force acts on it?

This time m and a are given. Multiply them.

🎬 Exam layout, step by step
Answer: F = ma = 10 000 kg × 1.5 m/s² = 15 000 N (= 1.5 × 10⁴ N).

6. Newton's third law: forces come in pairs

Stand on a small boat and push another boat away: both boats move, in opposite directions. Newton's third law: when object A pushes or pulls object B, B pushes or pulls A with a force of the same size in the opposite direction. The two forces act on different objects, so they never cancel each other.

🧪 Two boats on the backwaters

The push lasts 0.5 s; after that the boats glide (water drag is ignored). The two red arrows are always the same length – equal forces. The lighter boat gets the bigger acceleration (a = F/m) and moves away faster.

⭐ Why this matters for gravitation

The Earth pulls a falling coconut, and the coconut pulls the Earth up with exactly the same force. The coconut moves visibly; the Earth does not. Its mass is so enormous that a = F/m is practically zero. You will calculate this on page 8.
