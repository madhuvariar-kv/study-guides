Mass, Units and the Spring Balance മാസ് · സ്പ്രിങ് ബാലൻസ്

Physics numbers always come with a unit. “The pull is 9.8” means nothing; “the pull is 9.8 N” is physics. This page revises the SI units the chapter uses and shows how to convert grams to kilograms and km/h to m/s. It explains what mass is and how a common balance measures it, and how a spring balance measures a force. It ends with how to read a table and a line graph, which you need for Activity 4.1 and Table 4.1 of the chapter.

  • Force and the newton (page 1).
  • Multiplying and dividing by 10, 100, 1000.

1. SI units: the chapter's toolkit

Scientists all over the world use one set of units, the SI units. Every formula in this chapter (F = ma, F = G m₁m₂/d², g = GM/R²) gives the right answer only if you put the numbers in SI units.

Prefixes tell you how many times bigger or smaller: kilo (k) = 1000 times (1 km = 1000 m, 1 kg = 1000 g), centi (c) = one hundredth (1 cm = 0.01 m), milli (m) = one thousandth (1 mm = 0.001 m, 1 mg = 0.001 g).

⭐ Golden rule for numericals

Convert to SI units first, then substitute. Grams → kilograms, kilometres → metres, km/h → m/s, minutes → seconds. Most lost marks in this chapter come from a 100 g mass used as “100” instead of 0.1 kg, or a radius of 6400 km used as “6400” instead of 6 400 000 m.

2. Converting units

To convert, ask: is the new unit bigger or smaller? A kilogram is bigger than a gram, so you need fewer of them: divide by 1000. A metre is smaller than a kilometre, so you need more: multiply by 1000. For speed, 1 km/h means 1000 m in 3600 s, so 1 km/h = 1000/3600 m/s = 1/3.6 m/s: divide by 3.6 to go from km/h to m/s, and multiply by 3.6 to go back.

🧪 Conversion machines

Try 100 g (= 0.1 kg), 1.5 km (= 1500 m) and 36, 54, 72, 90, 108 km/h. They come out as 10, 15, 20, 25, 30 m/s.

Worked example 1 · A bus on the highway

Q. A KSRTC bus travels at 72 km/h. Express this speed in m/s.

1 km = 1000 m and 1 h = 3600 s. Replace each and simplify, or simply divide by 3.6.

🎬 Exam layout, step by step
Answer: 72 km/h = 72 × 1000 m ÷ 3600 s = 20 m/s. The bus covers 20 metres every second.

3. Mass: the amount of matter

The mass (മാസ്) of an object is the quantity of matter in it. A full sack of rice has more matter than a half-full one, so it has more mass. Mass is measured in kilograms (kg) and grams (g). Mass does not depend on where the object is: a coconut taken to the Moon still has the same amount of coconut in it.

Mass is measured with a common balance (കോമൺ ബാലൻസ്), a beam balance like the one in a jewellery shop or a grocery. It compares the object with standard masses: when the beam is level, the two pans hold equal masses.

🧪 Balance it! (common balance)

Add standard masses until the beam is level. Start with the biggest that does not tip the beam too far, then add smaller ones – just like a shopkeeper. Can you find the mass of the mystery stone?

🌍 Real life

A goldsmith's small beam balance compares gold with standard masses, and the old market balance (ത്രാസ്) with its hanging pans works the same way. Because a beam balance compares mass with mass, it would show the same result on the Moon – something you will explore on page 10.

4. How a spring balance works

Pull a spring gently and it stretches a little; pull twice as hard and it stretches twice as much. The stretch is proportional to the force. A spring balance (സ്പ്രിങ് ബാലൻസ്) uses this. A pointer fixed to the spring moves along a scale marked in newtons, so the reading is the force pulling the hook. Hang a mass on it and the force is the Earth's pull on that mass. For every kilogram, the Earth pulls with about 9.8 N:

reading in newtons = mass in kg × 9.8    (100 g → 0.1 × 9.8 = 0.98 N ≈ 1 N)
🧪 Spring balance lab

Try 100 g, then 200 g: the stretch doubles and the reading goes from 0.98 N to 1.96 N. On this scale one small division is 0.1 N (its least count). When the pointer stops between two marks, take the nearer one.

⚠️ Careful

A spring balance measures a force, not mass. Shop spring balances are often marked in “kg”, but that really means kilogram weight (കിലോഗ്രാം ഭാരം, kgwt), the Earth's pull on 1 kg, which is 9.8 N. You will meet this properly on page 10.

Worked example 2 · What is in the bag?

Q. A bag of sugar hung on a spring balance shows a reading of 2.45 N. What is the mass of the sugar? (Take the Earth's pull as 9.8 N for every kg.)

Reading = mass (kg) × 9.8. Divide the reading by 9.8, then change kg to g.

🎬 Exam layout, step by step
Answer: m = 2.45 ÷ 9.8 = 0.25 kg = 250 g.

5. Reading tables and line graphs

An experiment gives pairs of numbers, which we write in a table. A line graph turns the table into a picture: one quantity along the bottom (x-axis), the other up the side (y-axis), one dot for each pair. If the dots lie on a straight line through the origin, the two quantities are directly proportional (page 3).

🎬 Animation: drawing a graph from a spring-balance table

Not every graph is a straight line. In the chapter, Table 4.1 lists the Earth's pull on a 100 kg object at different heights. The dots there lie on a gentle curve that falls as the height grows. You read a curve the same way: go up from the x-value to the curve, then across to the y-axis.
