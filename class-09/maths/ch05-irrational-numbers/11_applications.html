Real-World Applications പ്രയോഗങ്ങൾ

Square roots are not just exam numbers. Paper sizes, TV screens, roof rafters, floor tiles, honeycombs and land surveys are all built on √2, √3 and Heron's formula. Every calculator below is something a real carpenter, surveyor or designer does.

📖 The three tools behind every application
  • Pythagoras gives a square root: a diagonal or a sloping length is √(a² + b²).
  • The rules √x × √y = √(xy) and √(x/y) = √x/√y let us simplify it: √50 = 5√2.
  • Heron's formula gives the area of any triangle from its three sides – no height needed.

1. A4 paper and √2 – the shape that survives folding

An A4 sheet is 210 mm × 297 mm. Divide: 297 ÷ 210 = 1.414… = √2. That is no accident. Fold an A4 sheet in half and you get A5, which has exactly the same shape. Only a 1 : √2 rectangle can do this.

🎬 Fold a 1 : √2 sheet
🎚️ The whole A series

Why √2? If the sides are 1 and x, halving gives sides x/2 and 1. Same shape means x ÷ 1 = 1 ÷ (x/2), so x² = 2 and x = √2. Each A size has half the area of the one before, and A0 has area exactly 1 square metre.

In the exam this looks like: "A rectangle has sides 1 and √2. Show that cutting it into two equal halves parallel to the shorter side gives rectangles of the same shape." → new sides √2/2 and 1; ratio 1 ÷ (√2/2) = 2/√2 = √2 ✓.

2. Screen diagonals: the hidden √337

A "55-inch TV" has a 55-inch diagonal. Almost every screen is 16 : 9, so width : height : diagonal = 16 : 9 : √(16² + 9²) = 16 : 9 : √337. One square root tells you the width of every screen ever sold.

📺 Screen calculator (16 : 9)
In the exam this looks like: "A screen is 16 units wide and 9 units high. Find its diagonal in simplest form." → √(256 + 81) = √337 (no perfect-square factor, so it stays as √337 ≈ 18.36).

3. Cutting a tile along its diagonal

Floor tilers cut square tiles diagonally to fill the triangular gaps along a wall. A square of side a has diagonal a√2 – the carpenter's "1.414 rule". The same number gives the diagonal of a square room, a square field, or a square window.

🔲 Diagonal of a square
In the exam this looks like: "A square tile of side 30 cm is cut along its diagonal. Find the length of the cut." → 30√2 ≈ 42.4 cm. "A square room has side 6 m. How far is it from one corner to the opposite corner?" → 6√2 ≈ 8.5 m.

4. Roof rafters: rise, run and √(rise² + run²)

A roof is a triangle. The horizontal half-width is the run, the height is the rise, and the sloping beam, the rafter, is the hypotenuse. Carpenters compute the rafter length before cutting a single piece of wood.

🏠 Rafter calculator
In the exam this looks like: "A roof rises 3 m over a horizontal distance of 6 m. Find the length of the sloping side in simplest form." → √(9 + 36) = √45 = 3√5 ≈ 6.71 m.

5. Surveying a plot of land with Heron's formula

A surveyor cannot measure the "height" of an irregular field, but can measure every side and a few diagonals with a tape. Split the plot into triangles from one corner, use Heron's formula on each, and add. This is exactly the textbook's സ്ഥലപ്പരപ്പ് box.

📐 Plot calculator – drag the corners

Lengths are in metres (1 small grid square = 1 m). Each triangle's area comes from Heron's formula with s = half its perimeter. Drag any orange corner and watch every number update.

In the exam this looks like: "A field ABCD has AB = 6 m, BC = 8 m, AC = 10 m, CD = 13 m, DA = 13 m. Find its area." → ΔABC is right-angled (6-8-10): 24 sq m; ΔACD: s = 18, √(18 × 8 × 5 × 5) = 60 sq m; total 84 sq m.

6. Honeycombs: why bees choose hexagons

Suppose a bee has a fixed length of wax wall (the perimeter) to build one cell. Which shape holds the most honey – a triangle, a square or a regular hexagon? The areas are all square-root formulas: equilateral triangle (√3/4)a², square a², regular hexagon (3√3/2)a².

🐝 Same perimeter, different shapes
In the exam this looks like: "Find the area of a regular hexagon of side 2 cm." → 6 equilateral triangles: 6 × (√3/4) × 4 = 6√3 ≈ 10.39 sq cm.

7. A tent with equilateral faces

A square-based tent (or the Great Pyramid in miniature) with four equilateral triangular faces of side a: each face has height (√3/2)a, so the canvas needed is 4 × (√3/4)a² = √3 a². And the tent's own height, from the ground to the tip, turns out to be a/√2 = a√2/2.

⛺ Tent calculator
In the exam this looks like: "A tent has four equilateral triangular faces of side 3 m. How much canvas is needed?" → 4 × (√3/4) × 9 = 9√3 ≈ 15.6 sq m.

8. Will the pole fit? The space diagonal of a room

The longest straight line inside a box-shaped room goes from a floor corner to the opposite ceiling corner. Pythagoras twice: floor diagonal √(a² + b²), then the space diagonal √(a² + b² + c²).

📦 Room diagonal
In the exam this looks like: "Can a straight 7 m pole be kept inside a room 5 m long, 4 m wide and 3 m high?" → space diagonal √(25 + 16 + 9) = √50 = 5√2 ≈ 7.07 m > 7 m, so yes, just.

9. No calculator? Squeeze the square root

Before calculators, people found √n by squeezing: pick a guess, square it, see whether it is too small or too big, and move closer. Every digit of 1.414… was found this way. Try it as a game.

🎯 Squeeze game
In the exam this looks like: "Between which two consecutive whole numbers does √40 lie?" → 6² = 36 < 40 < 49 = 7², so between 6 and 7 (closer to 6: 6.3² = 39.69, 6.4² = 40.96).

🧭 What to remember
  • A diagonal or sloping length is a square root (Pythagoras); simplify it with √(k²m) = k√m and keep the exact form until the last step.
  • Equilateral triangles hide √3; squares hide √2; hexagons are six equilateral triangles.
  • For any triangle whose sides you can measure, Heron's formula gives the area – that is how land is surveyed.
