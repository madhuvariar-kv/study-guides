Real-World Applications പ്രയോഗങ്ങൾ

Similar triangles are not just a textbook idea. They let you measure things you cannot reach – the height of a coconut tree, the width of a river, the size of the Sun – using only a measuring tape and a little thinking. Every simulator below is a real method people use.

📖 The one rule behind every application

Whenever two triangles have the same angles (usually a right angle plus one shared angle), their sides are in the same ratio. Find the two triangles, pair the sides correctly, write one equation, solve. That is the whole game.

1. Measuring a tree with its shadow (the method of Thales)

About 2600 years ago the Greek mathematician Thales visited Egypt and measured the height of the Great Pyramid without climbing it. He waited until his own shadow was exactly as long as his height – at that moment the pyramid's shadow was exactly as long as the pyramid was tall! You can do better: at any time of day, a stick and a tree standing in the same sunlight make two triangles with the same angles.

☀️ Shadow simulator

The sun's rays are parallel, so both triangles have the same angle at the shadow tip and a right angle at the base. Same angles ⇒ height ÷ shadow is the same number for the stick and the tree. Change the time of day (sun elevation): the shadows change, the ratio does not.

In the exam this looks like: "A 1.5 m stick casts a 2 m shadow while a tree casts a 16 m shadow. Find the height of the tree." → tree = 1.5 × 16 ÷ 2 = 12 m.

2. The mirror on the ground

No sun? Put a small mirror on the ground and walk backwards until you see the top of the building in it. Light bounces off a mirror at the same angle it arrives (angle of incidence = angle of reflection), so the two triangles at the mirror have the same angles.

🪞 Mirror simulator (eye height 1.5 m)

Move the mirror; the person walks back until the treetop appears in it. Height of tree = eye height × (mirror-to-tree) ÷ (mirror-to-person).

In the exam this looks like: "A mirror is 16 m from a building. A student whose eyes are 1.5 m above the ground sees the top of the building when standing 2 m from the mirror." → height = 1.5 × 16 ÷ 2 = 12 m.

3. Maps and zooming

A map is a similar copy of the land, reduced by the map's scale. When you pinch-zoom a phone map, every distance on the screen is multiplied by the same number – the triangle formed by three places keeps its shape, only the scale changes.

🗺️ Zooming a sketch map of Ernakulam district

Straight-line distances are approximate. Notice that the three map lengths change together while the three real distances stay fixed – that is exactly "sides in the same ratio".

In the exam this looks like: "On a map of scale 1 cm : 5 km two towns are 3.6 cm apart. Find the real distance." → 3.6 × 5 = 18 km.

4. Projectors, cinema screens and the pinhole camera

A projector lamp is a point of light. Light from the lamp passes through the small slide and spreads out in straight lines to the screen. The slide and the screen image are similar shapes, and the scale factor is simply (lamp-to-screen) ÷ (lamp-to-slide). Move the screen back and the picture grows – that is why a cinema needs a long hall!

📽️ Projector: slide 2 cm tall, 10 cm from the lamp

Figure not to scale. The two triangles (lamp–slide and lamp–screen) share the angle at the lamp and both have a right angle, so image ÷ slide = screen distance ÷ slide distance.

📷 Pinhole camera: the image is upside-down but similar

A 30 cm tall candle in front of a 10 cm deep box. The two triangles at the pinhole have equal (vertically opposite) angles, so image height ÷ object height = box depth ÷ object distance.

In the exam this looks like: "A slide 2 cm tall is 10 cm from a projector lamp. How tall is the picture on a screen 3 m away?" → 2 × 300 ÷ 10 = 60 cm.

5. Ladders, ramps and stairs

A ladder leaning on a wall makes a right triangle with the wall and the ground. Any point on the ladder, together with its foot and the point directly below it, makes a smaller right triangle with the same angles. So the height you reach is always the same fraction of how far you have climbed. Stair steps work the same way – every step has the same rise ÷ run as the whole staircase.

🪜 A 5 m ladder: foot 3 m from the wall, top 4 m up
In the exam this looks like: "A 6 m ladder reaches 5.5 m up a wall. How high is a person who has climbed 3 m along it?" → 5.5 × 3 ÷ 6 = 2.75 m.

6. How wide is the river? (no boat needed)

Surveyors measure across rivers, valleys and busy roads with a right-angle trick. Follow the steps.

🎬 The surveyor's method
In the exam this looks like: a figure with two right triangles touching at a point, vertically opposite angles marked – "find AT". Pair the sides opposite equal angles and write one ratio.

7. Why a solar eclipse is possible – the Sun and the Moon

The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon, but it is also about 400 times farther away. The two triangles from your eye to the edges of the Moon and to the edges of the Sun have the same angles, so the two discs look almost exactly the same size in the sky. That is why the Moon can cover the Sun perfectly in a total solar eclipse – a beautiful cosmic coincidence explained by similar triangles.

🌑 Eclipse simulator (not to scale)

Real numbers: Sun diameter ≈ 1 392 000 km at ≈ 149 600 000 km; Moon diameter ≈ 3 474 km at ≈ 384 400 km. Diameter ÷ distance is about 1/107 for the Sun and 1/111 for the Moon – nearly equal!

8. Enlarging a drawing: the pantograph

Artists and engineers once used a pantograph – a set of hinged rods – to copy a drawing at 2 or 3 times its size. Its secret: a fixed pivot O, and every point P of the drawing is sent to the point P′ on the ray OP with OP′ = k × OP. Triangles OPQ and OP′Q′ then have a common angle at O and two sides scaled by k – the "third way" of this chapter – so every length of the copy is k times the original. Your textbook's GeoGebra tool "Enlarge from Point" does exactly this.

🖐️ Enlarge from a point – drag the orange tracing point P
In the exam this looks like: "The lines joining a point P to the vertices of a triangle are extended to 1½ times their length. Prove the new triangle's sides are 1½ times the old." (Exercise 3, question 3.)

9. Scale models: building a 1 : 50 house

Architects build models before they build houses. A scale of 1 : 50 means every real length is divided by 50 (equivalently the scale factor is 1/50). Because all lengths shrink by the same factor, every triangle in the roof of the model has the same angles as the real roof – the model looks right.

🏠 Real house → model
In the exam this looks like: "A model of a building is made to the scale 1 : 50. The model's door is 4 cm tall. Find the real door height." → 4 × 50 = 200 cm = 2 m.

10. Try these word problems

🧭 The recipe for every application
  1. Draw the two triangles. Mark the right angles and the equal angle (shared, vertically opposite, alternate, or from parallel sun-rays).
  2. Pair the sides that face equal angles (or pair by size: short ↔ short, long ↔ long).
  3. Write one ratio equation: unknown / partner = known / partner.
  4. Cross-multiply and write the answer with its unit.
