📖 The ideas behind every lab
  • A gas at low pressure conducts electricity at high voltage. Cathode rays (electrons) travel from the cathode, make a coating glow, and are deflected by electric and magnetic fields (page 4).
  • Bohr: an electron that jumps to a higher orbit absorbs energy, and when it falls back it gives out energy (page 7).
  • Isotopes have the same atomic number but different mass numbers. Some are radioactive റേഡിയോ ആക്ടീവത: they give out radiation by themselves (Table 1.6, page 10).

1. Glowing signboards, street lamps and tube lights

At night, the shops in a Kerala town light up with red-orange neon signs. A neon sign is a discharge tube ഡിസ്ചാർജ് ട്യൂബ്: a glass tube with an electrode at each end, filled with a little neon gas at low pressure, and run on a high voltage, just like Crookes’ tube. Electrons rush from the cathode and crash into the gas atoms. Each collision lifts an electron of the atom to a higher energy level (it absorbs energy). When the electron falls back, it gives out that energy as light. Every gas gives its own colour: neon is red-orange and sodium vapour is yellow-orange, like the old street lamps. In a tube light or CFL, mercury vapour gives out invisible ultraviolet light, which makes the white coating on the inside of the glass glow go further.

💡 Discharge-lamp lab – pick a gas and switch on

Colours are approximate. A real signboard tube is bent into letters, and the voltage is made by a transformer inside the sign.

📝 In the exam this looks like… “Neon signboards work on the same principle as the discharge tube. Under what two conditions does a gas conduct electricity in a discharge tube?”
The gas must be at a very low pressure and a very high voltage must be applied across the electrodes. At normal pressure air is an insulator വൈദ്യുതരോധി and no current flows.

2. The old CRT television at grandma's house

Before flat screens, every TV and computer monitor had a big glass cathode ray tube (CRT) at the back, which is why the old TV at grandma's house is so deep. At the narrow end, a hot cathode gives out electrons, and an anode at high voltage speeds them into a thin beam: these are cathode rays. Just as in Fig 1.4, electric plates (or magnetic coils) bend the beam up and down and from side to side, so that it sweeps the screen line by line. The inside of the screen is coated with a substance that glows where the electrons hit it, like the zinc sulphide in Crookes’ tube. The beam paints the whole picture many times every second, so your eyes see a steady picture.

📺 CRT lab – paint a picture with cathode rays

Here the beam paints 16 lines very slowly. A real TV painted 625 lines, 25 times every second. Colour TVs used three beams and three kinds of glowing dot (red, green and blue) go further.

📝 In the exam this looks like… “Which property of cathode rays makes it possible to steer the beam over the screen of a CRT television? What is the charge of these particles?”
Cathode rays are deflected by electric and magnetic fields, so plates or coils can steer the beam anywhere on the screen. They bend towards the positive plate, so the particles are negatively charged (electrons). They also make the coating on the screen glow.
⚠ Never open an old TV, computer monitor or tube light. A CRT can hold a dangerous high voltage even after it is unplugged, its glass can implode, and tube lights and CFLs contain poisonous mercury. Broken ones go to e-waste collection, not into the dustbin.

3. Radioactive isotopes in hospitals

Table 1.6 lists three medical uses. Iodine-131 is used to study and treat the thyroid gland. The thyroid in the neck collects iodine from the blood, so after a patient swallows a tiny dose, a detector outside the neck “sees” how much the gland has taken up. A larger dose destroys an overactive gland or thyroid cancer from the inside. Cobalt-60 gives out powerful gamma rays that are used to treat cancer. The machine turns around the patient, so the tumour receives the rays from every side while the healthy skin gets only a little from each direction. Cancer hospitals such as the Regional Cancer Centre (RCC), Thiruvananthapuram have used cobalt-60 machines for many years. Iron-59 helps to diagnose anaemia: doctors follow how fast the body takes iron into new red blood cells.

🏥 Hospital lab – choose an isotope
📝 In the exam this looks like… “Write one use each of cobalt-60 and iodine-131.” “Which isotope is used to diagnose anaemia?”
Cobalt-60: treatment of cancer. Iodine-131: study and treatment of the thyroid gland. Iron-59 is used to diagnose anaemia (Table 1.6).

4. Carbon-14: how old is this fossil?

Plants take in carbon dioxide, and animals eat plants. So every living thing contains carbon, and a tiny, fixed share of it is radioactive carbon-14, 146C (8 neutrons). While the plant or animal is alive, the share stays the same, because it keeps taking in fresh carbon. After death no new carbon comes in, and the carbon-14 slowly breaks down. Every 5730 years about half of what is left is gone go further. By measuring how much carbon-14 is left in a bone, a piece of wood or charcoal, scientists find its age ഫോസിലുകളുടെ കാലപ്പഴക്കം. Archaeologists use this at excavation sites such as Pattanam in Ernakulam district, where charcoal and wood help to date the remains of an ancient port.

🦴 Dating lab – years since death and carbon-14 left

Carbon dating works for remains up to about 50 000 years old. Dinosaur fossils are millions of years old, so they are dated with other radioactive isotopes found in the rocks around them go further.

📝 In the exam this looks like… “Which isotope of carbon is used to find the age of fossils? How many neutrons does it have? Name the other two isotopes of carbon.”
Carbon-14, 146C, with 14 − 6 = 8 neutrons. The other isotopes are carbon-12 (6 neutrons, the most abundant) and carbon-13 (7 neutrons, about 1.1 %, used to study life processes).

5. Uranium-235 and heavy water in nuclear power stations

Uranium-235, 23592U (92 protons, 143 neutrons), is the fuel in nuclear power stations (Table 1.6). When a slow neutron hits a uranium-235 nucleus, the nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei, gives out two or three new neutrons and releases a huge amount of heat. This is called nuclear fission go further. The heat boils water, and the steam turns a turbine that makes electricity. Heavy water ഘനജലം is D₂O, the oxide of deuterium (hydrogen with 1 proton and 1 neutron). It slows the neutrons down so that they can split the next uranium nuclei, and control rods soak up the extra neutrons to keep the reaction steady. Most of India’s nuclear power stations, for example at Tarapur, Rawatbhata and Kaiga, use heavy water in this way go further. Kudankulam in Tamil Nadu uses ordinary water. Kerala has no nuclear power station.

⚛️ Reactor lab – a steady chain of fission
📝 In the exam this looks like… “What is heavy water? Where is it used?” “Which isotope of uranium is used as fuel in nuclear power stations? Find its number of neutrons.”
Heavy water is the oxide of deuterium, D₂O. It is used in nuclear power stations. The fuel is uranium-235: neutrons = 235 − 92 = 143.

6. Sodium-24: finding a leak in a buried pipe

Factories and cities carry oil, water and chemicals in pipes buried under the ground. To find a leak without digging up the whole road, engineers add a very small amount of a liquid containing radioactive sodium-24 to the pipe. Where the pipe leaks, the liquid soaks into the soil and the sodium-24 collects there. A worker walks along the road with a radiation detector, and the reading jumps right above the leak. Sodium-24 loses half of its radioactivity in about 15 hours, so within a few days almost none is left go further.

🔎 Leak-hunter lab – walk the detector along the road
📝 In the exam this looks like… “Name the radioactive isotope used to detect leaks in industrial pipelines, and explain how the leak is found.”
Sodium-24. A little of it is added to the liquid in the pipe. At the leak the liquid with sodium-24 collects in the soil, so a radiation detector moved along the ground above the pipe shows the highest reading at the leak.

7. Radiation safety – the ☢ symbol

The radiation from radioactive isotopes can damage the cells of living things, which is why the same gamma rays that kill a tumour must be kept away from healthy people. Radioactive isotopes are therefore handled only by trained people, in special laboratories, with shielding. The warning sign is a black trefoil on a yellow background. Wherever you see it, it means: radiation, keep away. Of the three radiations in the radioactivity box (α, β, γ), alpha is stopped by a sheet of paper, beta by a few millimetres of aluminium, and gamma only by thick lead or concrete go further.

🛡️ Shielding lab – what stops each radiation? go further
📝 In the exam this looks like… “Radioactive isotopes are very useful. Why are they handled only by trained people?”
The radiation they give out can harm living cells. So trained people handle them with shielding (lead, concrete) and special equipment, keep their distance and limit the time they are exposed.
