Matter, Elements, Atoms and Molecules

Your textbook begins with a table of familiar substances (പദാർഥം) – sugar, glucose and water. For each one it lists the constituent elements (ഘടക മൂലകങ്ങൾ), the chemical formula (രാസസൂത്രം) of its molecule (തന്മാത്ര) and the ratio of the numbers of atoms (ആറ്റങ്ങളുടെ എണ്ണത്തിന്റെ അനുപാതം). This page builds all of that from zero. You will see what matter is made of, and how elements, compounds and mixtures differ. You will learn what atoms and molecules are, learn the symbols of the elements in this chapter, and practise reading a formula and counting its atoms.

📖 What you need
  • Nothing from earlier classes – every idea is built up here.
  • Simple counting, and dividing two numbers by the same number (for ratios).

1. Everything is made of tiny particles

Anything that has mass and takes up space is matter: the water in your glass, the air you breathe, a gold chain, a grain of sugar. Pour water out drop by drop. Every drop is still water. If you could keep dividing a drop into smaller and smaller parts, you would finally reach a particle so small that it cannot be divided any further and still be water. That particle is a molecule of water (തന്മാത്ര). Molecules are themselves made of even smaller particles called atoms (ആറ്റങ്ങൾ).

🎬 Zoom into a glass of water

How many molecules are in one drop? A drop of water (about 0.05 mL) holds roughly 1.7 × 1021 molecules (approx.) – about 1 700 000 000 000 000 000 000. Page 3 explains how to read numbers like this.

2. Elements, compounds and mixtures

Look at the particles a substance is made of, and you can sort it into one of three groups:

GroupWhat its particles are likeExamples
Element മൂലകംMade of only one kind of atom. The atoms may be single (helium) or joined in molecules of the same atoms (O2).Hydrogen, oxygen, carbon, gold, iron, helium
Compound സംയുക്തംMade of two or more different elements joined together in a fixed ratio. All its molecules are alike.Water (H2O), carbon dioxide (CO2), sugar, glucose, common salt
Mixture മിശ്രിതംTwo or more substances simply mixed, not joined. You can change the amounts, and each part keeps its own properties.Air, salt water, sand with iron filings, sugar syrup
🧪 Element, compound or mixture? Look at the particles
Your answer:

Ask two questions. (1) Are all the particles the same? If not, it is a mixture. (2) If they are all the same, does each particle contain only one kind of atom (element) or different kinds joined together (compound)? (A drawing, not to scale; real particles are far too small and too many to show.)

3. Atoms and molecules

An atom (ആറ്റം) is the smallest particle of an element. Atoms of one element are alike. Atoms of different elements are different: a hydrogen atom is not the same as an oxygen atom. There are just over a hundred elements, so there are just over a hundred kinds of atoms. Everything around you is built from them, like all the words in a dictionary built from a few letters.

Atoms usually join together to make molecules. Your textbook defines a molecule like this:

⭐ Molecule – textbook page 8 തന്മാത്ര

A molecule is the smallest particle of a substance that has all the properties of the substance and can exist independently.

  • A molecule of an element has atoms of only one kind: oxygen gas is O2 (two oxygen atoms), hydrogen gas is H2.
  • Some elements exist as single atoms: helium (He), neon (Ne) and argon (Ar) gases. Their atom is their molecule.
  • A molecule of a compound has atoms of different elements: a water molecule has 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom.

Your textbook says molecules of different substances differ in two ways: (i) the constituent elements (ഘടക മൂലകങ്ങൾ) – which kinds of atoms are in them, and (ii) the ratio of the numbers of atoms (ആറ്റങ്ങളുടെ എണ്ണത്തിന്റെ അനുപാതം). Water (H2O) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) have the same elements but a different ratio, so they are completely different substances. You drink one; the other is a bleach!

🤔 And inside the atom?

For a long time people thought atoms could not be broken. This chapter tells the story of how scientists found still smaller particles inside the atom – the electron, proton and neutron, called subatomic particles (സബ്അറ്റോമിക കണങ്ങൾ).

4. Symbols of the elements

Chemists write each element as a short symbol (പ്രതീകം) of one or two letters. The rules are simple:

  • The first letter is always a capital; a second letter, if there is one, is always small: Ca, Cl, Co – never CA or cl.
  • Usually the symbol comes from the English name: H for hydrogen, C for carbon, O for oxygen.
  • When two names start with the same letter, a second letter is added: C carbon, Ca calcium, Cl chlorine, Co cobalt, Cu copper.
  • A few symbols come from the old Latin names: Na (natrium) for sodium, K (kalium) for potassium, Fe (ferrum) for iron, Cu (cuprum) for copper, Ag (argentum) for silver, Au (aurum) for gold.
⚠ Capitals matter

Co is the element cobalt. CO is a compound, carbon monoxide: one C atom and one O atom. A wrong capital changes the meaning completely.

5. Reading a chemical formula

A chemical formula (രാസസൂത്രം) shows which atoms are in one molecule and how many of each:

  • Each symbol stands for one atom of that element.
  • A small number written below and to the right of a symbol (a subscript) tells how many atoms of that element there are: in CO2, the 2 belongs to O, so there are 2 oxygen atoms.
  • No subscript means one atom: the C in CO2 is one carbon atom.
  • The ratio of the numbers of atoms compares the counts: in CO2, carbon : oxygen = 1 : 2.

The colours are only a code: real atoms have no colour. The ball models are flat drawings; real molecules are three-dimensional.

Worked example · Count the atoms in ethanol

Q. The alcohol in sanitisers is ethanol, C2H5OH. (a) Which elements are in it? (b) How many atoms of each are in one molecule? (c) How many atoms in all? (d) What is the ratio C : H : O?

Read the formula one symbol at a time. H appears twice – in H5 and again at the end (OH). Add both.

🎬 Step by step
Answer: (a) carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. (b) C = 2, H = 5 + 1 = 6, O = 1. (c) 2 + 6 + 1 = 9 atoms. (d) C : H : O = 2 : 6 : 1. (It is often written C2H6O.)

Simplifying a ratio

Like a fraction, a ratio can be simplified by dividing every number by the same number. In ethene, C2H4, the ratio C : H = 2 : 4. Divide both by 2 and you get 1 : 2. Your textbook does this for glucose on page 7, as you will see on page 4.

Try it. Hydrogen peroxide is H2O2. Write the ratio H : O and simplify it.

H : O = 2 : 2 = 1 : 1 (divide both by 2).
⭐ Key ideas
  • All matter is made of tiny particles. Molecules are made of atoms, and atoms have still smaller particles inside them.
  • Element (മൂലകം): one kind of atom. Compound: different elements joined in a fixed ratio. Mixture: substances just mixed.
  • A molecule (തന്മാത്ര) is the smallest particle of a substance that has all its properties and can exist independently.
  • A symbol (പ്രതീകം) has a capital first letter and a small second letter. A few come from Latin: Na, K, Fe, Cu, Ag, Au.
  • In a formula, a subscript counts the atoms of the symbol just before it; no subscript means one.
