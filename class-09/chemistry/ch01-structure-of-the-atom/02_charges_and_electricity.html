Charges, Electrodes and Low-pressure Gases

The electron and the proton were discovered with discharge tubes (ഡിസ്ചാർജ് ട്യൂബ്). These are glass tubes with metal electrodes (ഇലക്ട്രോഡുകൾ), a gas at low pressure and a high voltage. To follow those experiments you need a few ideas about electricity. This page covers positive and negative charge (ചാർജ്), and why like charges repel while unlike charges attract. It explains electric current and which electrode is the cathode (കാഥോഡ്) and which is the anode (ആനോഡ്). It shows why air is an insulator (വൈദ്യുതരോധി) and what pumping out the air does. Finally, it shows how charged plates and magnets bend a moving charged particle.

📖 What you need
  • Matter is made of tiny particles; atoms and molecules (page 1).
  • Nothing else – every idea is built up here.

1. Two kinds of charge

Comb your dry hair on a dry day and the comb picks up tiny bits of paper. Rub a balloon on your hair and the hair stands up towards it. These effects are caused by electric charge (ചാർജ്). There are only two kinds of charge, called positive (+) and negative (−).

  • Ordinary matter contains equal amounts of + and − charge, so the charges cancel. Such an object is neutral (uncharged).
  • When two things are rubbed together, some tiny negative particles move from one to the other. You will meet these particles as electrons on page 4. The positive charges stay where they are.
  • The object that gains negative particles becomes negatively charged. The one that loses them is left with extra + charge, so it becomes positively charged.
🧪 Rub a balloon on your hair

Each rub moves 2 negative charges from the hair to the balloon (in a real balloon it is billions of them). Count the signs: the balloon ends up with more − than +, and the hair with more + than −. Opposite charges attract, so the hair rises towards the balloon.

Like charges repel, unlike charges attract

Two objects with the same kind of charge push each other away: they repel (വികർഷിക്കപ്പെടുക). Two objects with opposite charges pull each other together: they attract. This simple rule explains a large part of this chapter. Cathode rays bend towards a positive plate, alpha particles bounce back from a positive nucleus, and electrons are held to the nucleus.

🧪 Two charged balls on threads

Think. A glass rod rubbed with silk becomes positively charged. Did the glass rod gain or lose negative particles?

It lost negative particles (electrons) to the silk. The positive charges do not move, so losing negatives leaves the rod with more + than −. The silk, which gained them, becomes negative.

2. Electric current, terminals and electrodes

An electric current is a flow of charge. In a metal wire, the moving charges are the tiny negative particles (electrons). A battery or other source has two terminals, marked + (positive) and − (negative). When a complete path joins them, the negative particles flow out of the − terminal, round the circuit, and back into the + terminal.

The metal plates or rods through which the current enters or leaves a material, a liquid or a gas are called electrodes (ഇലക്ട്രോഡുകൾ):

Materials that let current flow easily are conductors (വൈദ്യുതചാലകം): metals such as copper, iron and aluminium, and graphite (pencil lead). Materials that do not let current flow are insulators (വൈദ്യുതരോധി): plastic, rubber, glass, dry wood, and dry air at normal pressure.

🧪 Conductor or insulator? Test it between two electrodes

The dots show the negative particles drifting from the battery's − terminal, into the cathode, through the material, out of the anode and back to the + terminal. (Go further: by an old convention, current is drawn from + to −; the electrons really move from − to +.) A 4.5 V torch battery is safe to use.

3. Making a gas conduct: low pressure and high voltage

The air in a room is an insulator. Otherwise every wall socket would spark into the air around it! Two things change that:

  • A very high voltage (ഉയർന്ന വോൾട്ടത). Voltage is the electrical “push” of a source: a torch cell gives 1.5 V and a house socket in Kerala 230 V. A discharge tube needs thousands of volts.
  • Air at low pressure (കുറഞ്ഞ മർദത്തിലുള്ള വായു). A suction (vacuum) pump (നിർവാത പമ്പ്) removes most of the air from the tube. With fewer particles in the way, charged particles can travel far enough to gain energy and keep a current going.

When a current passes through a gas, the gas usually glows. This is called an electric discharge (വൈദ്യുത ഡിസ്ചാർജ്). Neon signboards in a Kerala town at night work this way: neon gas at low pressure glows red-orange when a high voltage is applied.

🧪 Pump the air out of a tube and switch on the high voltage

In this simple model each stroke of the pump removes half of the air that is left, so after n strokes the pressure is (½)n of normal. After 20 strokes it is about one-millionth. Each dot stands for an enormous number of particles, and the changes happen at approximate pressures. The anode has a hole in the middle, like the perforated anode in your textbook's Fig 1.1. Watch the three stages described on pages 8–9.

⚠ Discharge tubes run at thousands of volts – a teacher demonstration only. Never open or break tube lights, CFLs, neon signs or old CRT televisions: they contain mercury vapour or very high voltages.
⛈️ Kerala connection – lightning

During the April–May thunderstorms in Kerala, clouds build up huge charges. The voltage between a cloud and the ground can reach many millions of volts (approx.). That push is so large that even air at normal pressure breaks down and conducts for an instant. The result is a lightning flash. A discharge tube does the same thing gently, with a few thousand volts, by first removing most of the air.

🤔 Try it at home – “pumping” air out of a bottle

Suck the air out of an empty plastic water bottle and hold your tongue over the mouth. The bottle crumples. You have lowered the pressure inside, and the air outside pushes harder than the thin air inside. A vacuum pump does the same thing much better, stroke after stroke. (Use a plastic bottle only, never glass.)

4. Pushing a moving charge sideways

A charged particle that flies through certain regions is pushed off its straight path. Your textbook uses two such regions:

  • An electric field (വൈദ്യുതമണ്ഡലം): the space between two metal plates, one charged + and the other −. A negative particle is pulled towards the + plate; a positive particle bends towards the − plate; a neutral particle goes straight.
  • A magnetic field (കാന്തിക മണ്ഡലം): the space near a magnet. A magnet pushes a moving charge sideways, at right angles to its motion, so its path curves. Positive and negative charges curve in opposite directions. A neutral particle, or a charge that is not moving, is not pushed at all.
🧪 Beam bender – plates and magnets

A stronger field bends the path more. Too strong a field between the plates makes the particles hit a plate. On page 4 you will use exactly these tests to show that cathode rays are negative. (The amount of bending also depends on the particle's mass and speed; the picture is qualitative.)

⭐ Key ideas
  • There are two kinds of charge (ചാർജ്): positive and negative. Neutral = equal amounts of both.
  • Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.
  • The cathode (കാഥോഡ്) is the electrode joined to the − terminal; the anode (ആനോഡ്) is joined to the + terminal. Negative particles leave the cathode and travel towards the anode.
  • Air at normal pressure is an insulator (വൈദ്യുതരോധി). With a high voltage and low pressure a gas conducts and glows: an electric discharge.
  • Between charged plates a negative particle bends towards the + plate. A magnet pushes a moving charge sideways; + and − curve opposite ways.
