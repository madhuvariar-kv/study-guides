Inside the Atom: the Discharge Tube and the Electron ഡിസ്ചാർജ് ട്യൂബ് പരീക്ഷണങ്ങളും ഇലക്ട്രോണിനെ കണ്ടെത്തലും

Everything around you – sugar in your chaya, the water in the well, the air you breathe – is made of atoms. But what is inside an atom? On this page you count the atoms in three familiar molecules, then switch on a glowing glass tube, just as William Crookes did in 1875, and follow the clues (a shadow, a turning wheel, a bending beam) that led to the first particle smaller than an atom: the electron.

📖 What you need (pages 1–3 of this guide)
  • An atom (ആറ്റം) is the smallest particle of an element; atoms join to make molecules (തന്മാത്ര). A formula like H₂O tells you which atoms and how many (page 1).
  • Like charges repel, unlike charges attract. The cathode (കാഥോഡ്) is the negative electrode (−), the anode (ആനോഡ്) the positive one (+). Air at normal pressure is an insulator (വൈദ്യുതരോധി) (page 2).
  • Powers of ten: 10¹¹ = 1 followed by 11 zeros; 10⁻³¹ = 1 divided by 10³¹ (page 3).

2. Molecules, atoms and still smaller particles

⭐ Key idea (textbook page 8)

ഒരു പദാർഥത്തിന്റെ എല്ലാ ഗുണങ്ങളുമുള്ളതും സ്വതന്ത്രാവസ്ഥയിൽ നിലനിൽക്കാൻ കഴിയുന്നതുമായ ഏറ്റവും ചെറിയ കണികയാണ് തന്മാത്ര.

A molecule (തന്മാത്ര) is the smallest particle of a substance that has all the properties of that substance and can exist independently.

വിവിധ പദാർഥങ്ങളിലെ തന്മാത്രകൾ എങ്ങനെയെല്ലാം വ്യത്യാസപ്പെട്ടിരിക്കുന്നു? – How do the molecules of different substances differ?

  • in the constituent elements they contain (തന്മാത്രയിലടങ്ങിയിരിക്കുന്ന ഘടക മൂലകങ്ങൾ) – water has H and O, sugar has C, H and O;
  • in the ratio of the numbers of atoms of those elements (ഘടക മൂലക ആറ്റങ്ങളുടെ എണ്ണത്തിന്റെ അനുപാതം) – sugar 12 : 22 : 11, glucose 1 : 2 : 1.

Molecules are made of atoms. You learnt in earlier classes that atoms in turn contain still smaller particles. ആറ്റങ്ങളിൽ അടങ്ങിയിരിക്കുന്ന പ്രധാന കണങ്ങൾ ഏതൊക്കെയാണെന്ന് അറിയാമോ? – Do you know the main particles in an atom? The book gives the first: electron (ഇലക്ട്രോൺ), ……, ……

• electron ഇലക്ട്രോൺ  • proton പ്രോട്ടോൺ  • neutron ന്യൂട്രോൺ
These are called subatomic particles (സബ്അറ്റോമിക കണങ്ങൾ) – particles smaller than an atom. This page is the story of the first one to be found, the electron; the proton and neutron follow on pages 5 and 6.

3. Crookes' discharge tube (Fig 1.1)

In 1875 the scientist William Crookes took a glass tube with a metal plate – an electrode (ഇലക്ട്രോഡുകൾ) – sealed in at each end and passed electricity through it at a very high voltage (ഉയർന്ന വോൾട്ടത). Such a tube is a discharge tube (ഡിസ്ചാർജ് ട്യൂബ്). A side tube leads to a vacuum (suction) pump (നിർവാത പമ്പ്) so that the air inside can be pumped out bit by bit.

Air is an insulator (വൈദ്യുതരോധി), so at normal pressure no electricity flows through the air in the tube. But when the pressure is reduced step by step to a very low value, a current flows through the tube – an electric discharge (വൈദ്യുത ഡിസ്ചാർജ്). With a perforated anode (സുഷിരങ്ങളുള്ള – with holes), a pale-green glow (ഇളംപച്ച നിറത്തിലുള്ള തിളക്കം) appears on the glass wall behind it, which is coated with zinc sulphide (സിങ്ക് സൾഫൈഡ് (ZnS)). The glow is caused by rays coming from the cathode, so they were named cathode rays (കാഥോഡ് രശ്മികൾ).

⚠ A discharge tube works at many thousands of volts – it is a teacher's demonstration only. Never open an old tube light, CFL or a CRT television: they contain mercury or work at high voltage, and the glass can implode.

Pressures are rough values – the exact pressure at which each change happens depends on the tube and the voltage. Notice that the colour of the gas glow at medium pressure depends on the gas – that is why neon signs glow orange-red.

🕰 Timeline – from glowing gases to the electron
💡 Why did it take so long?

Scientists knew in the early 1800s that electricity can pass through gases at low pressure, and Michael Faraday studied the changes it produced. But there were no good suction pumps, and it was hard to make glass tubes and pump the air out of them. Only after Heinrich Geissler built better tubes and pumps in 1854 could the experiments really begin. Science often waits for better tools!

5. The electron and J. J. Thomson

Changing the gas in the tube or the metal of the electrodes made no difference to the nature of the rays. So the particles in cathode rays are present in all substances. These particles are the electrons (ഇലക്ട്രോൺ).

J. J. Thomson measured the ratio of the charge (ചാർജ്) to the mass (മാസ്) of the electron – the e/m ratio (e/m അനുപാതം). When the scientific world accepted his work on cathode rays, it became clear that particles smaller than atoms exist. For the discoveries that followed from the discharge tube experiments, Thomson received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1906.

📦 Box – Mass of the electron (ഇലക്ട്രോണിന്റെ മാസ്, page 10)

The e/m ratio of the electron is 1.76 × 10¹¹ C/kg. But J. J. Thomson could not find the charge and the mass separately. Later, Robert Millikan, through his famous oil-drop experiment (ഓയിൽ ഡ്രോപ്പ് പരീക്ഷണം), found that the electron has a negative charge of 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, and from this calculated the mass of the electron as 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg. (C = coulomb കൂളോം, the unit of charge.)

🧮 Step by step: from e/m and e to the mass of the electron
💡 Go further – how did Millikan weigh a charge?

Millikan sprayed tiny oil drops between two charged plates and watched one drop through a microscope. By adjusting the voltage he could make a charged drop hang still: the electric pull up balanced its weight down. The charges on the drops always came out as whole-number multiples of one smallest charge, 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C – the charge of one electron.

6. Questions from the textbook (page 10)

Q1. ഇലക്ട്രോണിന് മാസുണ്ടെന്ന് തെളിയിച്ചതെങ്ങനെ? – How was it proved that the electron has mass?

A light paddle wheel placed in the path of cathode rays turns (and rolls towards the anode). Only particles that have mass (and are moving) can push the paddles, so the particles of cathode rays – the electrons – have mass. Why light can't do it: a beam of torchlight shone on the same wheel does not turn it.

Q2. കാഥോഡ് രശ്മികളുടെ പാതയിൽ ഒരു അതാര്യ വസ്തു വച്ചാൽ നിഴൽ ഉണ്ടാകുന്നു. ഇതിൽ നിന്ന് എന്ത് മനസ്സിലാക്കാം? – An opaque object placed in the path of cathode rays casts a shadow. What does this show?

Cathode rays travel in straight lines. Why: a sharp shadow with the same shape as the object forms only if the rays go straight past its edges, just as sunlight makes a sharp shadow of a coconut tree.
🌴 Kerala connection – the old TV at grandma's house

The big, deep television sets of the 1990s were cathode ray tubes (CRTs). At the narrow back end, a hot cathode shot out a beam of electrons; magnets (coils) bent the beam left-right and up-down hundreds of times a second, and wherever it struck the coated screen it glowed – painting the picture line by line. The colourful signboards of a Kerala town at night are discharge tubes too: neon glows orange-red, other gases and coatings give other colours (page 13).

🧭 Summary
  • A molecule is the smallest particle of a substance that has all its properties and can exist independently. Molecules differ in their elements and in the ratio of their atoms.
  • Atoms contain smaller subatomic particles: electrons, protons and neutrons.
  • Discharge tube (Crookes, 1875): no current at normal pressure (air is an insulator); at very low pressure and high voltage, cathode rays from the cathode make the ZnS-coated glass glow pale green.
  • Cathode rays travel in straight lines (shadow), have mass (paddle wheel), are negative (bend towards +) and are bent by a magnet; they are the same for every gas and metal → electrons are in all matter.
  • Thomson measured e/m = 1.76 × 10¹¹ C/kg (Nobel Prize 1906); Millikan found e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; so the mass of an electron = 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg.
