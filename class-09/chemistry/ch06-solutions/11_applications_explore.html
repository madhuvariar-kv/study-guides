1. Cleaning paint brushes: turpentine, not water

like dissolves like At the science fair the enamel paint on Rahul's hands would not wash off with water, but a little turpentine took it off at once. Enamel paint, grease and tar are non-polar നോൺപോളാർ; water is polar പോളാർ. Turpentine, kerosene and petrol are non-polar organic solvents ഓർഗാനിക് ലായകങ്ങൾ, so they dissolve paint and grease. That is why painters clean brushes in turpentine and mechanics wipe grease with kerosene, and why salt and sugar, which are polar, dissolve in water but not in kerosene.

🖌️ Dip a painted brush
📝 In the exam this looks like… “A painter uses turpentine, not water, to clean brushes used for enamel paint. (a) What is the solute and the solvent here? (b) Explain the reason.”
(a) Solute ലീനം: enamel paint; solvent ലായകം: turpentine. (b) Like dissolves like: enamel paint is non-polar and dissolves in a non-polar solvent such as turpentine. Water is polar, so it cannot dissolve the paint.
⚠ Turpentine and kerosene are flammable and their vapours are harmful. Use them only with an adult, in an open, airy place, far from any flame; wear gloves and wash hands with soap afterwards.

2. Saline drips and ORS: mass percentage in hospitals go further

mass percentage The “drip” given in hospitals is often normal saline: a solution of common salt in very pure water, about 0.9 % salt. ORS (oral rehydration solution), given when a person loses water and salts through diarrhoea, is a solution of salts and glucose made in exactly the amount of clean water printed on the packet. In both, the amount of solute in a definite amount of solution, the concentration ലായനിയുടെ ഗാഢത, must be correct: too dilute or too concentrated would not help the body. These are medicines: they are prepared and given as a doctor or health worker advises.

💧 How much salt in a saline bottle?

Strictly, hospital saline is labelled 0.9 % w/v (0.9 g salt per 100 mL). For such a dilute solution 100 mL has a mass very close to 100 g, so the mass percentage is practically the same.

📝 In the exam this looks like… “Normal saline contains 0.9 % sodium chloride by mass. (a) How much sodium chloride is in 500 g of saline? (b) How much water?”
(a) Mass of solute = 0.9 × 500 ÷ 100 = 4.5 g of NaCl. (b) Water = 500 − 4.5 = 495.5 g. Check: 4.5 ÷ 500 × 100 = 0.9 % ✔.

3. Bleaching solution and chlorine in drinking water: % and ppm

mass % · ppm The bleaching solution sold for cleaning contains 3.62 % sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl): 3.62 g in every 100 g of solution. When the amount of solute is extremely small, percentage gives awkward numbers, so we use parts per million (ppm) പാർട്സ് പെർ മില്ല്യൺ (ppm): parts of solute in 10⁶ (one million) parts of solution. Water authorities add a tiny amount of chlorine to drinking water to kill germs; the textbook gives about 4 ppm. One litre of water has a mass of about 1 kg = 1 000 000 mg, so 4 ppm means about 4 mg of chlorine in 1 L.

🚰 4 ppm chlorine – how much is that?
📝 In the exam this looks like… “(a) Drinking water contains 4 ppm chlorine. What is the mass of chlorine in 1000 kg of this water? (b) A bleaching solution is 3.62 % NaOCl. How much NaOCl is in 500 g of it?”
(a) 4 ppm = 4 g in 10⁶ g. 1000 kg = 10⁶ g, so the chlorine is 4 g. (b) 3.62 × 500 ÷ 100 = 18.1 g of NaOCl.
⚠ Bleaching solution irritates skin and eyes. Never mix it with acids or with other cleaners (a poisonous gas can form). Keep it locked away from children and never drink or taste it.

4. Kalkandam: sugar crystals from a supersaturated syrup

supersaturation · crystallization Kalkandam (sugar candy), given as prasadam in temples, is made from a supersaturated അതിപൂരിത ലായനി sugar syrup. A lot of sugar is dissolved in hot water, the syrup is cleaned and then left to cool slowly and undisturbed with threads hanging in it. As it cools, the syrup holds more sugar than it can at that temperature; the extra sugar comes out as big, clear crystals on the threads over several days. This is crystallization പരലീകരണം, just like the growing copper sulphate crystal on page 5. go further About 200 g of sugar saturates 100 g of water at 20 °C, but nearly 490 g near 100 °C.

🍬 Cool slowly and wait

illustrative times

📝 In the exam this looks like… “Sugar candy (kalkandam) is made by cooling a hot, concentrated sugar syrup slowly. (a) What kind of solution is formed on cooling? (b) Name the process by which the crystals form. (c) Why should the syrup not be disturbed?”
(a) A supersaturated solution: it holds more sugar than needed to saturate it at the lower temperature. (b) Crystallization. (c) A supersaturated solution is unstable; disturbing it makes the excess sugar come out suddenly as many tiny crystals instead of slowly building a few large ones.
⚠ Hot sugar syrup sticks to the skin and causes bad burns. Let an adult handle the boiling syrup.

5. Fish gasping on a hot afternoon: oxygen in warm water

gas solubility · temperature Fish breathe the oxygen dissolved in water. The solubility of a gas decreases as the temperature rises: from the textbook graph (Let us assess, Q9) oxygen falls from about 0.0068 g to 0.004 g per 100 g of water between 0 °C and 20 °C, and to about 0.003 g at 40 °C. On a hot summer afternoon, in a shallow pond or a fish tank in the sun, the water holds less oxygen, so fish come up to the surface and gasp. Hot water released from factories, and rotting waste that uses up oxygen, make it worse. go further The graph values are for water in contact with pure oxygen; water in contact with air holds less, but it follows the same trend.

🐟 A pond through the day
📝 In the exam this looks like… “On hot summer days, fish in a pond come to the surface to breathe. Give the reason, using the idea of solubility.”
The solubility of gases in water decreases as the temperature increases. On hot days the pond water is warm, so less oxygen is dissolved in it. The fish do not get enough oxygen in the water and come to the surface, where the water is in contact with air.

6. Soda: carbon dioxide under pressure

gas solubility · pressure In a soda factory, carbon dioxide is dissolved in water at high pressure and the bottle is sealed. In the closed bottle there are no bubbles. When the cap is opened the pressure falls to the air pressure; the water can no longer hold so much gas, and CO₂ escapes as bubbles. A warm soda fizzes over and goes flat quickly, because a gas is less soluble at a higher temperature; that is why the juice shop keeps soda bottles in the fridge. The factor that keeps the gas in the soda is pressure.

🥤 Open the bottle
📝 In the exam this looks like… “When a soda bottle is opened, a lot of bubbles come out. (a) Which gas is it? (b) Which factor affecting solubility is involved? (c) Why does a warm soda go flat faster?”
(a) Carbon dioxide. (b) Pressure: CO₂ was dissolved at high pressure; on opening, the pressure falls, the solubility decreases and the extra gas escapes. (c) The solubility of a gas decreases as the temperature rises, so warm soda holds less CO₂ and loses it quickly.

7. “Shake well before use”: syrup or suspension?

suspension A clear cough syrup is a true solution യഥാർഥ ലായനി: its particles are tiny and never settle, so it needs no shaking. Ayurvedic kashayam, some antacids and calamine lotion are suspensions സസ്പെൻഷൻ: their particles are larger than 1000 nm, so they slowly settle to the bottom. The label “shake well before use” makes sure every dose has the same amount of medicine.

💊 Two bottles on the shelf
📝 In the exam this looks like… “Which of these bottles should carry the label ‘Shake well before use’: a clear cough syrup, ayurvedic kashayam, tincture of iodine? Give the reason.”
Ayurvedic kashayam. It is a suspension: its particles are big enough to settle on standing, so it must be shaken to spread them evenly. Clear cough syrup and tincture of iodine are true solutions; their particles never settle.

8. Mist on the Munnar road: the Tyndall effect

colloid · Tyndall effect Drive up to Munnar or Wayanad on a winter night and the headlight beams look like solid white cones in the mist (മൂടൽമഞ്ഞ്). Mist and clouds are colloids: tiny water droplets (liquid) spread in air (gas), a liquid aerosol. The droplets are the right size (1–1000 nm and a little larger) to scatter light, so the path of the beam becomes visible. This is the Tyndall effect ടിൻഡൽ പ്രഭാവം. On a clear night the beam itself is invisible: we see only the lit road. The same effect shows sunbeams through the trees of a misty forest, the beam of a cinema projector in a dusty hall and light in smoke.

🌫️ Headlights on a hill road
📝 In the exam this looks like… “The beams of headlights are clearly seen on a misty night but not on a clear night. (a) Name this phenomenon. (b) Explain. (c) Give one more example.”
(a) Tyndall effect. (b) Mist is a colloid: tiny water droplets dispersed in air. These colloidal particles scatter the light, so the path of the beam is seen. Clear air has no such particles, so the beam is not seen. (c) Sunbeams through gaps in trees or a window into a dusty room; the beam of a cinema projector; a torch beam through milky water.
⚠ Fog is dangerous on hill roads. Drivers should slow down and use low beam (high beam is scattered back into their eyes by the mist).

9. Milk, curd, butter and ghee: colloids in the kitchen

types of colloids Milk is a colloid: tiny drops of fat (liquid) spread through water (liquid), an emulsion. Churning curd or cream makes the fat drops join into butter, in which tiny drops of water are held in solid fat; the textbook lists butter, jam and cheese as gels (liquid dispersed in a solid). When butter is heated to make ghee, the water boils off and the milk solids are removed, so ghee is almost pure butterfat and no longer a colloid. go further Curd is also a gel: the milk proteins form a soft network that traps the water.

🔬 Zoom into the kitchen
📝 In the exam this looks like… “Milk is a colloid. (a) Which type of colloid is it? (b) Name the dispersed phase and the dispersion medium. (c) Name a colloid in which a liquid is dispersed in a solid.”
(a) Emulsion. (b) Dispersed phase ഡിസ്പേർസ്ഡ് ഫേസ്: liquid (fat drops); dispersion medium ഡിസ്പേർഷൻ മീഡിയം: liquid (water). (c) A gel: butter, jam or cheese.

10. Reading the label on a bottled drink

additives · stabilisers · preservatives Every packed food in India must carry a label with the ingredients, the date of manufacture and “best before”, a veg/non-veg mark and the FSSAI licence number (the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India). Additives are often written with their INS code numbers. Using Table 6.5 of the textbook you can recognise the colours (tartrazine – yellow, erythrosine – red, indigo carmine – blue), the preservatives പ്രിസർവേറ്റീവുകൾ (sodium benzoate, sorbic acid) and the stabilisers സ്റ്റെബിലൈസറുകൾ (such as glyceryl ester of rosin) that keep the particles of an artificial drink കൃത്രിമ പാനീയങ്ങൾ from settling.

📝 In the exam this looks like… “The label of a soft drink shows: tartrazine, sodium benzoate, glyceryl ester of rosin. Write the purpose of each.”
Tartrazine: a colour (yellow). Sodium benzoate: a preservative; it acts against yeast and moulds so the drink does not spoil. Glyceryl ester of rosin: a stabiliser; it keeps the particles in the drink from settling so the drink stays uniform.

11. 916 gold in Thrissur's jewellery shops: a solid solution

solid solution · mass percentage Pure gold is too soft for ornaments, so jewellers mix it with a little silver and copper. A 916 hallmark means 916 g of pure gold in 1000 g of the ornament (91.6 %); the rest is silver and copper. The metals are melted together and mixed evenly, so the ornament is a solid solution ഖരലായനി, an alloy ലോഹസങ്കരം, with gold as the solvent. go further Purity is also given in carats out of 24: 916 gold is 22 carat (22 ÷ 24 ≈ 0.917); 750 gold is 18 carat.

💍 1000 parts of an ornament
📝 In the exam this looks like… “A chain of 916 gold has a mass of 8 g. (a) What is the mass of pure gold in it? (b) Which is the solvent in this solid solution?”
(a) 916 gold has 916 g gold in 1000 g, so gold = 8 × 916 ÷ 1000 = 7.328 g (the other 0.672 g is silver and copper). (b) Gold, the component present in the largest amount.
🔑 One chapter, many uses

Choose the right solvent: like dissolves like (turpentine for paint, water for salt). Measure concentration: mass percentage for bleach, saline and gold, ppm for chlorine. Use solubility: cool a supersaturated syrup slowly for crystals; keep soda cold and closed; remember warm water holds less oxygen. Know the mixture: suspensions settle (“shake well”), colloids scatter light (mist, milk), stabilisers keep drinks uniform.
