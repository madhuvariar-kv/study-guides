1. Percent means “out of 100”

Per cent means “per hundred”. 34 % means 34 parts out of every 100 parts. Brass contains 34 % zinc (page 3): in every 100 g of brass there are 34 g of zinc and 66 g of copper. The two percentages add up to 100 %, because together they make the whole.

2. What percentage is the part of the whole?

Usually the whole is not exactly 100. Then we work out what the part would be if the whole were 100:

percentage = (part ÷ whole) × 100

In this chapter the “whole” is often made of two parts added together, for example solute + solvent = solution. Then you must add first to find the whole, and only then divide.

3. Ratios

A ratio compares two amounts. “Salt and water in the ratio 1 : 9” means 1 g of salt for every 9 g of water. The whole is 1 + 9 = 10 parts, so salt is 1 out of 10 parts = 10 % of the mixture, and water is 90 %. To turn a ratio into a percentage, add the parts to get the whole first.

Try it. Brass has copper and zinc in the ratio 66 : 34. What percentage of brass is copper?

The whole is 66 + 34 = 100 parts, so copper is 66 % and zinc 34 %. Here the ratio parts already add up to 100, so they are the percentages.

4. Units: grams, kilograms and millilitres

  • 1 kg = 1000 g and 1 g = 1000 mg (milligrams). To change kg into g, multiply by 1000; to change g into kg, divide by 1000.
  • 1 L = 1000 mL. Volume is measured in millilitres (mL) with a measuring cylinder.
  • Water is special: 1 mL of water has a mass of about 1 g (its density is 1 g/mL). So 100 mL of water weighs about 100 g, and 800 g of water takes up about 800 mL. The chapter uses this often (“100 mL of water” = 100 g of solvent).

5. Reading a line graph

A line graph shows how one quantity changes when another changes. Before reading it, look at four things:

  1. the horizontal axis (x-axis): what is measured along the bottom, and its unit (here: time in minutes);
  2. the vertical axis (y-axis): what is measured up the side, and its unit (here: temperature in °C);
  3. the scale: how much each small square stands for;
  4. the shape of each line: rising (going up to the right), falling (going down) or flat (hardly changing). A steep line changes fast; a gentle one changes slowly.

To read a value: start at the number on the x-axis, go straight up to the line, then straight across to the y-axis and read the number there. On page 6 you will read solubility curves in exactly this way.

6. Very small sizes: powers of ten and the nanometre

Scientists write very big and very small numbers with powers of ten. 10³ = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000, and 10⁶ = 1 000 000 (one million, used in ppm on page 4). For small numbers the power is negative: 10⁻³ = 1 ÷ 1000 = 0.001.

  • 1 millimetre (mm) = 10⁻³ m – the smallest division on your ruler.
  • 1 micrometre (µm) = 10⁻⁶ m = one thousandth of a millimetre.
  • 1 nanometre (nm) = 10⁻⁹ m = one millionth of a millimetre. So 1000 nm = 1 µm.

⭐ Key ideas
  • Percentage = (part ÷ whole) × 100. If the whole is “part + rest”, add them first.
  • A ratio 1 : 9 means 1 part out of 10 parts = 10 %.
  • 1 kg = 1000 g · 1 L = 1000 mL · for water, 1 mL ≈ 1 g.
  • Read a graph: up from the x-value to the line, then across to the y-axis. Rising, falling, flat; steep means a fast change.
  • 10⁶ = one million · 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m · 1000 nm = 1 µm.
⚠️ Common mistakes
  • Dividing by the rest instead of the whole: 10 g in 90 g of water is not 10 ÷ 90; the whole is 100 g, so it is 10 %.
  • Forgetting the units on the axes: “30” on a solubility graph means 30 g per 100 g of water, not 30 °C.
  • Thinking a bigger negative power means a bigger size: 10⁻⁹ m is much smaller than 10⁻⁶ m.
