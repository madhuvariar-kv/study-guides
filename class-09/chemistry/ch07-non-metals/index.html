Non-metals അലോഹങ്ങൾ

The chapter opens with the periodic table. Five boxes stand out from it: hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and chlorine. These are five important non-metals, and you will meet each one in turn. You will make hydrogen from zinc and acid and hear (virtually) its “pop”. You will see why it is a fuel with the highest calorific value, and also why it is hard to use. Carbon comes in many forms called allotropes: hard diamond, slippery graphite, one-atom-thick graphene and the football-shaped fullerene. Carbon dioxide turns lime water milky and puts out fires, while carbon monoxide is a silent poison. Nitrogen is the lazy gas of the air, held together by a strong triple bond, yet lightning and tiny root bacteria turn it into plant food. Oxygen keeps us alive, and its cousin ozone shields the Earth from ultraviolet rays. Chlorine bleaches cloth and makes drinking water safe. This self-study guide starts from the basics and explains every idea with animations and virtual labs. It answers every textbook question and ends with a revision sheet and a model test.

The chapter in one picture

🧪 Five non-metals light up · 💎 one element, three forms · 🌩️ lightning makes nitric oxide

Part 1: the five non-metals of this chapter (അലോഹങ്ങൾ) and their places in the periodic table (page 1). Part 2: diamond (വജ്രം), graphite (ഗ്രാഫൈറ്റ്) and fullerene (ഫുള്ളറീൻ) are all pure carbon. Different forms of the same element are allotropes (രൂപാന്തരങ്ങൾ, page 5). Part 3: the great heat of lightning makes nitrogen and oxygen combine, N₂ + O₂ → 2NO, the first step from air to the nitrates in the soil (page 7). Atoms are drawn far bigger than they really are.

⚠ Safety in this chapter: hydrogen burns explosively, so no flame is ever brought near a hydrogen generator except for the tiny test-tube “pop” test. Chlorine is a poisonous gas: it is made only by the teacher in a fume cupboard, and in this guide only as a virtual demonstration. Carbon monoxide is a silent poison: never burn charcoal or run an engine in a closed room. Heating potassium permanganate, concentrated acids and dry ice (it causes frostbite) are for the teacher only. Never smell a gas directly from a jar, and never taste any laboratory chemical.

The chapter at a glance

🎈 Hydrogen and 💎 carbon

  1. Hydrogen: the most abundant element in the universe · Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂↑ · burns with a “pop” (പോപ്പ് ശബ്ദം)
  2. Hydrogen as a fuel (ഇന്ധനം): highest calorific value (കലോറിക മൂല്യം), 143 kJ/g, and only water is formed
  3. Allotropes of carbon: crystalline (diamond, graphite, graphene, fullerene) and amorphous (charcoal, coke, lamp black, coal)
  4. Carbon dioxide: CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂ · turns lime water (ചുണ്ണാമ്പുവെള്ളം) milky
  5. Carbon monoxide is poisonous · carbonates (കാർബണേറ്റുകൾ): the barium chloride test

🌩️ Nitrogen, 🫁 oxygen and 🧴 chlorine

  1. Nitrogen: 78 % of air, inert (നിഷ്ക്രിയം) because of the N≡N triple bond · lightning · nitrogen fixation (നൈട്രജൻ സ്ഥിരീകരണം) · fertilisers
  2. Oxygen: 2KMnO₄ → K₂MnO₄ + MnO₂ + O₂ · a glowing splinter bursts into flame · supporter of combustion (ജ്വലനസഹായി)
  3. Ozone (O₃) absorbs ultraviolet radiations (അൾട്രാവയലറ്റ് വികിരണങ്ങൾ) · CFCs cause ozone layer depletion (ഓസോൺ പാളിയുടെ ശോഷണം)
  4. Chlorine: greenish-yellow and pungent · bleaches only when moist, by nascent oxygen (നവജാത ഓക്സിജൻ)
  5. Bleaching powder (ബ്ലീച്ചിങ് പൗഡർ) · the chloride test with silver nitrate

        Two scientists you will meet

        🎈

        Henry Cavendish

        1731–1810 · British scientist
        • Discovered hydrogen in 1766.
        • He called it inflammable air (കത്തുന്ന വായു) because it catches fire so easily.
        • The name hydrogen comes from the Greek word Hydrogenes, “water-producer”: burning hydrogen gives water.
        • Read his side box on page 3 (textbook page 134).
        🫁

        Joseph Priestley

        1733–1804 · English scientist
        • Discovered oxygen in 1774.
        • The French scientist Lavoisier gave it its name, from the Greek word oxygenes, “acid-producer”.
        • Oxygen is called പ്രാണവായു, the “life gas”.
        • Read his side box on page 8 (textbook page 146).

        Safety first

        ⚠️ This chapter has dangerous gases – read before any experiment
        • Hydrogen burns explosively when mixed with air. Keep flames away from any hydrogen generator; only the small test-tube “pop” test is done, with the teacher.
        • Chlorine is poisonous. Its preparation from potassium permanganate and concentrated hydrochloric acid (Fig 7.10) is a teacher demonstration in a fume cupboard; in this guide it is only virtual.
        • Carbon monoxide has no colour and no smell but is poisonous. Never burn charcoal or a stove in a closed room, and never run an engine in a closed garage.
        • Heating potassium permanganate (Fig 7.9), handling concentrated acids and dry ice (it causes frostbite) are for the teacher only.
        • Never smell a gas straight from a jar and never taste any laboratory chemical. Wear safety glasses when your teacher asks.
