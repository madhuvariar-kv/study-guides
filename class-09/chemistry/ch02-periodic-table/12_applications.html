Applications & Uses പീരിയോഡിക് ടേബിൾ

The periodic table is not only a chart on the classroom wall. The salt in your kitchen, the chalk on the blackboard and the bleaching powder in the water tank are all made from its elements. So are the neon signboard on the shop, the bell-metal ഓട് nilavilakku, the gold jewellery of Thrissur and the black sand of the Chavara beach. On this page every family മൂലക കുടുംബങ്ങൾ gets a short story, a small animation and a sample exam question.

📖 The idea behind every section
  • Elements in the same group have the same number of outermost electrons ബാഹ്യതമ ഇലക്ട്രോണുകൾ, so they show similar chemical properties രാസഗുണങ്ങൾ (page 5).
  • Noble gases ഉൽകൃഷ്ട വാതകങ്ങൾ have a stable outer shell of 8 (helium 2), so they normally do not react (page 7).
  • Transition elements സംക്രമണ മൂലകങ്ങൾ are all metals, and most of their compounds are coloured നിറമുള്ള സംയുക്തങ്ങൾ (page 7). Lanthanoids are also called rare earths റെയർ എർത്ത്സ് (page 8).

1. Alkali metals: the salt in the kitchen and the yellow street lamp

You never see pure sodium at home, but you eat it every day. Common salt is sodium chloride, NaCl. Sodium is so reactive that in nature it is always found joined with other elements. The yellow-orange street lamps on many highways are sodium vapour lamps: a little sodium vapour glows when electricity passes through it. Sodium, potassium and the other members of group 1, the alkali metals ആൽക്കലി ലോഹങ്ങൾ, all have just one outermost electron. They give it away easily, which is why they all react in the same lively way. Potassium is needed by plants, so it is in the “K” of the N-P-K fertiliser on a farmer's bag.

💡 One outer electron, one family
Lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium: every atom of group 1 has one outermost electron (shown in red).
📝 In the exam this looks like… “Sodium (Z = 11) and potassium (Z = 19) show similar chemical properties. Give the reason, and name the family they belong to.”
Na is 2, 8, 1 and K is 2, 8, 8, 1. Both have 1 outermost electron, so both are in group 1. The outermost electrons take part in chemical reactions, so elements with the same number of them show similar chemical properties. The family is the alkali metals.
⚠ Pure sodium and potassium react violently with water and can catch fire, so they are stored under kerosene. They are for teacher demonstration only. go further

2. Alkaline earth metals: bones, chalk and green leaves

Group 2, the alkaline earth metals ആൽക്കലൈൻ എർത്ത് ലോഹങ്ങൾ, are all around you. Calcium makes your bones and teeth strong. Chalk, limestone, marble and sea shells are all calcium carbonate, CaCO₃, the white compound of Table 2.10. In the green colour of every leaf, called chlorophyll, there is a magnesium atom right at the centre of each molecule. Without magnesium, a coconut palm cannot make its food. Calcium (2, 8, 8, 2) and magnesium (2, 8, 2) both have 2 outermost electrons.

🍃 Zoom into a leaf

The chlorophyll ring is drawn very simply: four nitrogen atoms hold the magnesium at the centre. go further

📝 In the exam this looks like… “Calcium (Z = 20) is present in bones and in limestone. Write its electron configuration and find its group and period. To which family does it belong?”
Calcium: 2, 8, 8, 2. 2 outermost electrons → group 2. 4 shells → period 4. Family: alkaline earth metals.

3. Groups 13 to 16: vessels, pencils, chips, fertilisers and oxygen

Many of the elements of the main groups പ്രധാനഗ്രൂപ്പ് മൂലകങ്ങൾ are in daily use. Aluminium vessels and foil come from group 13 (boron family). Pencil “lead” is really graphite, a form of carbon, from group 14 (carbon family). Computer chips and solar panels are made of silicon, a metalloid ഉപലോഹങ്ങൾ from the same group. Fertilisers carry nitrogen and phosphorus (group 15, nitrogen family). Hospitals keep oxygen cylinders (group 16, oxygen family). This one corner of the table has metals, non-metals and metalloids side by side, just as the key box says.

🧭 Pick an everyday thing and find its element
📝 In the exam this looks like… “Silicon (Z = 14) is used to make solar cells. Write its electron configuration and find its group and period. Is it a metal, a non-metal or a metalloid?”
Silicon: 2, 8, 4. 4 outermost electrons + 10 → group 14. 3 shells → period 3. It is a metalloid: it shows some properties of metals and some of non-metals.

4. Halogens: clean water, strong teeth and iodised salt

After the floods, health workers add bleaching powder to wells and water tanks. It releases chlorine, which kills germs. Swimming pools and town water supplies are made safe in the same way. Your toothpaste may say “contains fluoride”: compounds of fluorine help to make tooth enamel harder. The salt packet says “iodised”, which means a tiny amount of an iodine compound is added. Fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine are the halogens ഹാലൊജനുകൾ of group 17. Every one of them has 7 outermost electrons.

🔢 Count the outer electrons down group 17
Down the group the atoms get bigger (more shells), but the outer shell always holds 7 electrons.

Our body needs a little iodine to make the hormone of the thyroid gland, which is why salt is iodised. go further

📝 In the exam this looks like… “Fluorine (Z = 9) and chlorine (Z = 17) are in the same group. Write their configurations. How many outermost electrons do they have? Name the group number and the family.”
F: 2, 7; Cl: 2, 8, 7. Both have 7 outermost electrons, so the group is 7 + 10 = 17. The family is the halogens.
⚠ Chlorine gas is poisonous. Never mix bleaching powder or bleach with acids or other cleaners, and use only the amount the health worker tells you. Small children should not swallow toothpaste.

5. Noble gases: neon signs, argon bulbs and helium balloons

The red-orange glow of a shop's signboard comes from neon in a glass tube. Old filament bulbs are filled with argon. Argon does not react with the white-hot filament, while the oxygen of air would burn it out at once. Welders also use argon to shield hot metal from air. The balloons sold at temple festivals that float up are filled with helium. It is very light and, unlike hydrogen, it cannot catch fire. All of these uses depend on one property: noble gases ഉൽകൃഷ്ട വാതകങ്ങൾ have a stable സ്ഥിരത full outer shell, so they do not normally take part in chemical reactions.

⚡ Three noble gases at work
📝 In the exam this looks like… “Argon is filled in filament bulbs. Write the electron configuration of argon (Z = 18) and explain why it is used.”
Argon: 2, 8, 8. Its outermost shell has 8 electrons, a stable arrangement, so argon does not react, even with the white-hot filament. With air inside, the oxygen would react with the filament and burn it.

6. Transition metals in Kerala life

The middle block of the table is the busiest in a Kerala home. There is the copper or bell-metal uruli, the nilavilakku lit at dusk, the gold jewellery of the Thrissur shops, the galvanised iron roofing sheets and buckets, and the steel almirah. Two coloured compounds have jobs too. Deep-purple potassium permanganate disinfects wells after floods, and blue copper sulphate, mixed with lime as Bordeaux mixture, is sprayed on arecanut palms against the fungal disease മഹാളി (koleroga). Iron, copper, zinc, manganese, chromium, nickel, silver and gold are all transition elements: all metals, and most of their compounds are coloured.

🏠 Tap a thing at home – see its elements in the table
📝 In the exam this looks like… “Potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) is deep purple and copper sulphate is blue, but calcium carbonate is white. Explain.”
KMnO₄ contains manganese and copper sulphate contains copper, both transition elements. Most compounds of transition elements are coloured. Calcium carbonate contains no transition element (calcium is in group 2), so it is white.
⚠ Potassium permanganate is a strong oxidiser that stains skin and clothes, so use only the small amount the health officials advise for a well. Copper sulphate is harmful if swallowed, so wear gloves and a mask when spraying Bordeaux mixture and wash your hands afterwards. Never taste any chemical.

7. The black sand of Chavara

On the beaches of Chavara and Neendakara in Kollam, the sand has black and golden streaks. These are valuable minerals. The black ilmenite is a compound of iron and titanium. At KMML, Chavara, it is turned into titanium dioxide, the bright white pigment in paints, paper and plastics. The golden monazite contains the rare earths റെയർ എർത്ത്സ്, the lanthanoids ലാൻഥനോയ്ഡുകൾ of the separate row below the table. They are separated at IREL, Chavara, and used in very strong small magnets, as in motors and earphones, and in the glowing colours of screens and lamps. go further

🏖️ Separate the sand, find the elements

Ilmenite is slightly magnetic, so factories really do use magnets to separate it. The drawing is simplified. go further

📝 In the exam this looks like… “Rare earths are obtained from the monazite sand of Chavara. What name is given to these elements in the periodic table? Where are they placed?”
They are the lanthanoids, lanthanum (57) and the next 14 elements up to lutetium (71). They belong to period 6 but are placed in a separate row below the main table. With the actinoids they are called the inner transition elements.

8. Why chemists love the table: it predicts

A table that groups similar elements lets you predict. Mendeleev left gaps and described elements nobody had seen. One of them was “eka-aluminium”, the element just below aluminium. Four years later gallium was found, and its properties matched. The same idea works today. If you know that sodium and potassium are reactive metals with one outer electron, you can say that rubidium, just below them in group 1, will behave the same way, and even more vigorously.

🔮 Mendeleev's prediction against the real gallium go further
📝 In the exam this looks like… “Element X is in group 1, just below potassium. Predict its number of outermost electrons and one chemical property. Which family does it belong to?”
Same group as potassium → 1 outermost electron. It will react in the same way as sodium and potassium, for example it will react vigorously with water. Family: alkali metals. (X is rubidium, 2, 8, 18, 8, 1, in period 5.)
🧯 Safety first – chemistry at home
  • Never taste, smell closely or touch an unknown chemical. Many chemicals look like salt or sugar.
  • Bleaching powder, potassium permanganate and copper sulphate are useful but harmful. Use only the amount advised, wear gloves, and keep them away from children.
  • Never mix cleaning products. Some mixtures give off poisonous chlorine.
  • Reactive metals like sodium and potassium, and the chemicals of Table 2.10, are handled by the teacher only.
  • Old thermometers contain mercury, which is poisonous. If one breaks, do not touch the beads; tell an adult.
🔑 Take-away

Know an element's group and you know its family's behaviour. Same group, same number of outermost electrons, similar chemical properties. That is why one chart lets you understand table salt, bleaching powder, a neon sign and a gold chain.
