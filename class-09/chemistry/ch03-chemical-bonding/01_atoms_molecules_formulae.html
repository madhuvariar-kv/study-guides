Atoms, Molecules and Reading Formulae തന്മാത്ര

The chapter opens at a school science exhibition. The students stare at pictures of molecules in which the atoms are strung together like beads on a necklace (മാലയിലെ മുത്തുകൾപോലെ). What holds those beads together? That question is the whole chapter. Before you can answer it, you need a few basic tools. You need to tell an element from a compound, read a chemical formula and count the atoms in it, know which molecules have one, two or many atoms, and know which elements are metals and which are non-metals. This page builds those tools from zero and fills in Tables 3.1 and 3.2 of your textbook.

1. Atoms held together like beads

പദാർഥങ്ങളിലെ ഘടക കണങ്ങൾ തമ്മിലുള്ള ആകർഷണബലമാണ് അവയെ ചേർത്തു നിർത്തുന്നത്. – It is the force of attraction (ആകർഷണബലം) between the constituent particles (ഘടക കണങ്ങൾ) of a substance that holds them together.

Everything around you, and your own body, is made of tiny particles called atoms (ആറ്റം). Usually atoms do not float about alone. They join into groups called molecules (തന്മാത്ര). A molecule is the smallest particle of a substance that can exist on its own and still has the properties of that substance. A water molecule, for example, is one oxygen atom joined to two hydrogen atoms.

A necklace (മാല) stays together because a thread runs through the beads. In a molecule there is no thread. The atoms are held by a force of attraction between them, and this force is called the chemical bond (രാസബന്ധനം). This chapter is about how such bonds form and why.

🧵 Molecule necklace – pull the beads apart

The balls are a code: real atoms have no colours, and the sticks only show which atoms are joined. Pull the atoms apart and let go: the force of attraction pulls them back. Breaking a bond needs energy. The double and triple sticks in O₂, N₂ and CO₂ are explained on page 6.

3. Reading a chemical formula

A chemical formula (രാസസൂത്രം) tells you which atoms are in one molecule and how many of each. Learn these four reading rules:

  1. Each symbol stands for one atom of that element. A symbol starts with a capital letter: in NaCl, Na and Cl are the two symbols.
  2. A small number written low on the right is a subscript (പാദാങ്കം). It tells how many atoms of the symbol just before it. H₂O = 2 H + 1 O. No subscript means 1.
  3. A subscript after a bracket multiplies everything inside the bracket. Ca(OH)₂ = 1 Ca + 2 O + 2 H. (You will write formulas like this on page 10.)
  4. A big number in front counts molecules. 2H₂O means two water molecules: 4 H + 2 O = 6 atoms.

🤔 Is common salt made of NaCl molecules?

Not really. Solid common salt is a huge crystal of charged particles called ions, packed in the ratio 1 sodium : 1 chlorine. The formula NaCl gives that ratio, not a small separate molecule. You will see why on pages 4–5.

Think. Oxygen gas O₂ and ozone O₃ are both made of only oxygen atoms. Are they elements or compounds?

Both are elements: each contains only one kind of atom, oxygen. They differ only in how many atoms make one molecule (2 in O₂, 3 in O₃).

5. Metals and non-metals

When atoms bond, it matters a great deal whether they are metals (ലോഹങ്ങൾ) or non-metals (അലോഹങ്ങൾ). Metals are shiny, malleable and conduct electricity: sodium, magnesium, aluminium, potassium, calcium, iron, copper. Non-metals are usually dull, brittle and do not conduct: hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, sulphur, chlorine. Boron and silicon are metalloids (ഉപലോഹങ്ങൾ), with both natures.

A preview of the big idea: a metal + a non-metal usually join by giving and taking electrons (an ionic bond, pages 4–5). Non-metal + non-metal usually join by sharing electrons (a covalent bond, pages 6–7). Noble gases usually do not join at all (page 3).

⭐ Key ideas
  • The force of attraction (ആകർഷണബലം) between constituent particles (ഘടക കണങ്ങൾ) holds a substance together.
  • An element (മൂലകം) has one kind of atom; a compound (സംയുക്തം) has different elements joined in a fixed ratio.
  • In a formula, a subscript (പാദാങ്കം) counts the atoms of the symbol just before it; a subscript after a bracket multiplies everything inside; a number in front counts molecules.
  • Noble gases are monatomic; H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂ are diatomic (ദ്വയാറ്റോമിക തന്മാത്ര); H₂O, CH₄ and sugar are polyatomic.
  • Metal + non-metal → usually electron transfer; non-metal + non-metal → usually sharing.
