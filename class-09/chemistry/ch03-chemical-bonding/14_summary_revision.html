2. The key boxes of the textbook

1Octet configuration (p. 45)അഷ്ടകവിന്യാസം

Having 8 electrons in the outermost shell, like the noble gases, is called the octet configuration. (Helium: 2 – the duplet.) – page 3

2Chemical bond (p. 46)രാസബന്ധനം · ആകർഷണബലം

The force that holds the constituent particles together in a compound is the chemical bond. – page 3

3Ionisation energy (p. 47)അയോണീകരണ ഊർജം

The energy needed to remove the most loosely bound electron of the outermost shell of an isolated gaseous atom. – page 4

4Electron gain enthalpy (p. 48)ഇലക്ട്രോൺ ആർജിത എൻഥാൽപി

The energy released when an electron is added to a neutral gaseous atom to make it a negative ion. – page 4

5Ionic bond (p. 50)അയോണിക ബന്ധനം · ഇലക്ട്രോവാലന്റ് ബന്ധനം

In ionic compounds, the electrostatic force of attraction that holds the oppositely charged ions together is the ionic (electrovalent) bond. – page 4

6Ionic compounds (p. 51)അയോണിക സംയുക്തങ്ങൾ

Compounds formed by ionic bonds are ionic compounds or electrovalent compounds (NaCl, MgO). – page 5

7Covalent bond, single bond (p. 52)സഹസംയോജകബന്ധനം · ഏകബന്ധനം

The chemical bond formed by sharing electrons is the covalent bond. Sharing one pair gives a single bond: F–F, H–H. – page 6

8Double and triple bonds (p. 53)ദ്വിബന്ധനം · ത്രിബന്ധനം

Sharing two pairs → double bond (O=O); sharing three pairs → triple bond (N≡N). – page 6

9Covalent compounds (p. 55)സഹസംയോജക സംയുക്തങ്ങൾ

Compounds formed by covalent bonds are covalent compounds. Non-metals combining usually give covalent compounds (HCl, H2O). – page 7

10Electronegativity (p. 55)ഇലക്ട്രോനെഗറ്റിവിറ്റി

The relative ability of an atom in a covalent bond to attract the shared electron pair. Pauling scale 0–4; fluorine highest (3.98). – page 8

11Valency (p. 58)സംയോജകത

The number of electrons an atom gives, takes or shares when it takes part in a chemical reaction. – page 9

12Salts are neutral (p. 63)വൈദ്യുതപരമായി നിർവീര്യം

The total of the charges of the positive and negative ions in a salt is zero. – page 10

BoxGilbert Newton Lewis (p. 49)ഇലക്ട്രോൺ ഡോട്ട് ഡയഗ്രം

1875–1946, University of California: the electron pair, the covalent bond and electron dot structures; he also named the photon. – page 4

BoxPolar nature and shape (p. 57)പോളാർ സ്വഭാവം · ജ്യാമിതീയ ആകൃതി

CO2, CCl4 and BeF2 are non-polar although their bonds have large electronegativity differences – because of their shape the pulls cancel. – page 8

BoxHydrogen bonding (p. 58)ഹൈഡ്രജൻ ബന്ധനം

H bonded to F, O or N gets δ+ and is attracted by an electronegative atom of the same or another molecule. It makes water a liquid and a universal solvent; ice is less dense than water. – page 8

BoxIntermolecular forces (p. 58)അന്തർതന്മാത്രാബലങ്ങൾ

Forces of attraction between molecules – hydrogen bonding is one of them. – page 8

BoxVariable valency (p. 59)സംയോജകത

Fe 2 (FeCl2, ferrous) and 3 (FeCl3, ferric); Cu 1 (Cu2O, cuprous) and 2 (CuO, cupric); P 3 (PCl3) and 5 (PCl5). – page 9

The 1.7 rule ഇലക്ട്രോനെഗറ്റിവിറ്റി സ്കെയിൽ

Difference = larger value − smaller value (Fig 3.12). ≥ 1.7 → generally ionic; < 1.7 → covalent. In a covalent bond between different atoms the more electronegative atom gets δ− and the other δ+ (δ+H–Clδ−) – a polar bond. Equal atoms (H2, N2, O2) → non-polar.

Writing formulae – the criss-cross steps രാസസൂത്രം · പാദാങ്കം

  1. Write the symbols (or ions) – the metal / less electronegative one first: Al O.
  2. Write each valency (or charge number) above its symbol: Al 3, O 2.
  3. Swap them crosswise as subscripts: Al2O3.
  4. Divide by a common factor (Mg2O2 → MgO; C2O4 → CO2) and leave out 1.
  5. A radical taken more than once goes in brackets: Ca(OH)2, Mg3(PO4)2, (NH4)2SO4.
  6. Check: the total charge of a salt is zero.

Acids give H+ ions – their number is the basicity (HCl monobasic, H2SO4 dibasic, H3PO4 tribasic). Alkalis give OH− ions equal in number to the charge of the positive ion (NaOH, Ca(OH)2, Al(OH)3). Salts: acid + base → salt + water, e.g. NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H2O (നിർവീരീകരണ രാസപ്രവർത്തനം).

⚠️ Common mistakes
  • Changing the number of protons when an ion forms. Only electrons move: Na+ still has 11 protons, Cl− still 17.
  • Writing Na− or Cl+. Metal atoms lose electrons → positive ions; non-metals gain → negative ions.
  • Calling an ionic bond “sharing” or a covalent bond “transfer”. Ionic = transfer + attraction of ions; covalent = sharing of pairs.
  • Counting shared electrons wrongly. O=O shares 2 pairs = 4 electrons; N≡N shares 3 pairs = 6 electrons.
  • Drawing all the electrons in a dot diagram. Only the outermost electrons are drawn (Cl: 7, not 17).
  • Taking the 1.7 rule the wrong way round, or subtracting the bigger value from the smaller. Always larger − smaller; 1.7 or more is ionic.
  • Forgetting to simplify or to use brackets. Mg2O2 → MgO, C2O4 → CO2; write Mg3(PO4)2, not Mg3PO42.
  • Writing the more electronegative element first. NaCl, not ClNa – the metal (less electronegative) comes first.
  • Thinking every molecule with polar bonds is polar. CO2, CCl4, BeF2 are non-polar because of their shape.
  • Saying ionic compounds conduct as solids. Only when molten or dissolved – then the ions can move.
